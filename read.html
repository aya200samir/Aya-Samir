CHAPTER ONE

1.1 A Face in the Crowd

A person walking through a busy city centre does not ordinarily expect to become the subject of a technological identification process. They may pass a shop window, cross a street, or walk beneath a surveillance camera without giving the camera much thought. The presence of cameras in public spaces has become familiar. What is less immediately apparent is that a camera can be connected to a system capable of examining the faces of people passing before it and comparing them, within moments, against photographs held in a database.

That difference is the starting point of our inquiry.

A conventional surveillance camera records images. A facial recognition system does something additional: it processes those images to assess whether a face captured by the camera corresponds to a face represented in a collection of existing photographs. The process can take place automatically, at considerable speed, and without requiring the person being examined to cooperate.

The distinction matters because recording an image and attempting to establish a person's identity are not the same activity. The second involves a further use of information about the individual, with consequences that may extend beyond the camera itself.

In Cardiff, Wales, this distinction became the subject of a significant legal challenge.

The Technology Arrives in Cardiff

Between May 2017 and April 2019, South Wales Police conducted approximately fifty deployments of a live automated facial recognition system known as AFR Locate. The system was used at various public events as part of a programme of testing and operational deployment.

The term automated facial recognition, or AFR, describes technology designed to assess whether images of faces represent the same person. In the system used by South Wales Police, surveillance cameras captured facial images from a live video feed. Software processed the images and compared information extracted from them against photographs on a police watchlist.

A watchlist, in this context, was a collection of photographs selected by the police for comparison during a particular deployment. It was not necessarily a list of convicted criminals. According to the Court of Appeal's account, the categories used by South Wales Police could include people wanted on warrants, suspects, missing or vulnerable people, and others considered of interest for specified policing purposes.

That distinction is important. The presence of a person's photograph on a watchlist does not, by itself, establish that the person has committed an offence. Nor does a possible match between a camera image and a watchlist photograph establish criminal responsibility.

The system's immediate function was narrower: to identify a possible correspondence between facial images.

The legal significance of that correspondence depended on what happened next.

On 21 December 2017, South Wales Police deployed a marked vehicle equipped with facial recognition technology on Queen Street, a busy shopping area in Cardiff. The system operated from 8:00 a.m. until 4:00 p.m.

Three watchlists were used during that deployment. One concerned a person suspected of a serious crime. Another contained photographs of 382 people wanted on warrants. The third contained 536 suspects associated with the police area.

During the deployment, the system generated ten possible matches. According to the facts recorded by the Court of Appeal, eight were true matches and two were not. Two arrests followed the true matches.

Those figures require careful interpretation. They describe the outcomes reported for that particular deployment. They do not establish the system's overall accuracy, its performance across different populations, or the probability that any future alert would be correct. A reliable performance assessment would require substantially more information about the people scanned, the testing conditions, and the criteria used to classify outcomes.

For now, however, the figures illustrate a simpler point: an automated facial recognition system can produce both correct and incorrect possible matches.

The distinction between them is not merely technical. An incorrect alert may draw official attention to someone who is not the person being sought. A correct alert may identify someone whose presence is relevant to a legitimate investigation. In either situation, the significance of the alert depends on how it is interpreted and acted upon.

The second deployment relevant to the litigation occurred on 27 March 2018, during a defence exhibition at the Motorpoint Arena in Cardiff.

The police had particular security concerns about the event. Previous exhibitions had been associated with disorder, criminal damage, and false bomb reports. Facial recognition cameras were directed towards the arena's entrance.

The system operated between 8:30 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. Again, three watchlists were used. They included individuals connected with incidents at the previous year's event, people wanted on warrants, and suspects associated with offences in the police area.

On this occasion, the recorded results included one correct match and no false alerts. No arrests were made. The person correctly identified had previously been convicted in connection with a false bomb report relating to the event. Information about her presence was passed to the event commander, but no further action was taken.

This second deployment demonstrates another essential distinction: a technological identification does not necessarily lead to an arrest, a search, or even a conversation with the person concerned.

Between the system's output and any exercise of police powers, there may be human assessment, institutional procedures, and legal requirements.

Those intervening steps are part of the system's real-world operation. They cannot be ignored simply because the underlying technology is automated.

The Man Who Challenged the System

Edward Bridges was a civil liberties campaigner living in Cardiff. He challenged the legality of South Wales Police's use of AFR Locate, including its deployment on the two occasions described above.

Bridges stated that he had been present in Queen Street during the December 2017 deployment. He said that he had passed close to the police vehicle and had not seen signs warning him about the use of facial recognition before coming within range of the cameras.

He also stated that he attended a protest outside the Motorpoint Arena during the March 2018 deployment. According to his evidence, he was initially some distance from the facial recognition vehicle but came closer to it, and he had not been aware of the technology's use before seeing the vehicle.

There is an evidential qualification here that must not be overlooked.

South Wales Police could not subsequently verify whether Bridges's image had actually been recorded by the cameras or whether his facial biometric information had been processed on either occasion. For the purposes of the proceedings, the police accepted his account on a pragmatic basis and did not dispute his standing to bring the challenge.

This is not the same as a technical finding that the system had identified him.

Indeed, the litigation did not depend on proving that Bridges had been incorrectly matched with someone on a watchlist. His challenge concerned the lawfulness of the police's use of the technology and the interference with rights arising from that use.

This distinction helps us understand why the case matters.

A legal challenge to an artificial intelligence system does not always begin with an allegation that the system produced an inaccurate result. Sometimes the question arises earlier, when an institution decides to collect information, process it through a technological system, or exercise a power under procedures that may be insufficiently defined.

The absence of a demonstrated false identification does not, by itself, answer those questions.

What the Camera Could Do—and What It Could Not Decide

To understand the dispute, we must distinguish the different activities that were brought together within AFR Locate.

The camera captured images. Software detected faces within those images. The system extracted numerical information representing facial characteristics and compared that information with corresponding data derived from photographs on a watchlist.

When the comparison met a specified threshold, the software could generate an alert indicating a possible match.

A threshold is a predefined point used to decide whether a calculated result is sufficient to trigger a particular system response. Its role is similar, in a limited sense, to a qualifying score: a result on one side of the threshold is treated differently from a result on the other.

This analogy explains the decision rule, not the mathematics of facial recognition. The detailed meaning of a similarity score, the selection of a threshold, and the different forms of error will be examined later in this chapter.

For the moment, the essential point is that the alert was not itself a legal determination of identity.

Under the procedure described in the judgment, a police officer acting as the system operator reviewed the camera image alongside the relevant watchlist photograph. If the operator did not consider the images to depict the person being sought, no further action followed. If the operator believed there was a match, other officers could be informed and might approach the person or, where legally appropriate, exercise statutory powers.

The process therefore involved several distinct stages: automated comparison, human review, and possible police action.

Each stage raised different questions.

Was the person appropriately included on the watchlist? Was the system suitable for the conditions in which it was used? How were possible matches assessed? What information was available to the reviewing officer? Under what circumstances could an alert justify further intervention?

These questions cannot be answered simply by observing that the system employed facial recognition technology.

They require an understanding of the technology's purpose, its operation, the decisions made by the institution using it, and the legal rules governing those decisions.

The technology also operated on a scale that would have been difficult to achieve through ordinary human observation alone. The Court of Appeal recorded that AFR Locate was capable of scanning fifty faces per second, although this did not necessarily mean fifty different people. Across approximately fifty deployments in 2017 and 2018, around half a million faces may have been scanned.

Most of the people whose facial information was processed were not suspected of wrongdoing and were not otherwise of interest to the police.

This scale introduced a further issue. A system intended to locate a comparatively small number of people could process information about a much larger population in order to perform that task.

The distinction between the people being sought and the people being examined was therefore central to understanding the system's effects.

The Information That Disappeared—and the Processing That Had Already Occurred

South Wales Police's arrangements included measures for deleting certain information.

According to the judgment, where no match was generated, the facial image and biometric information processed by AFR Locate were deleted immediately and automatically. The underlying CCTV footage was retained separately for thirty-one days under the applicable surveillance arrangements. Information associated with a match could be retained within AFR Locate for up to twenty-four hours.

These details are significant because they demonstrate that data processing does not consist of a single event.

Information may be captured, transformed, compared, displayed, retained, or deleted at different points in a system's operation. Each activity has its own purpose and may raise its own legal questions.

Consider the position of a person whose face is scanned but produces no alert.

The system may delete the information derived from that person's face almost immediately. Nevertheless, the image has already been captured and processed for comparison.

Deletion may reduce certain risks associated with continued storage or later use. It does not mean that the preceding processing never occurred.

This is an important distinction for legal reasoning. The lawfulness of an activity cannot always be assessed solely by examining what information remains after the activity has finished.

It may also be necessary to ask what information was obtained, what operations were performed on it, why those operations were undertaken, and whether appropriate legal safeguards applied at the time.

Facial recognition makes these questions particularly significant because it involves biometric information: information produced through technical processing of characteristics of the human body or behaviour that can be used to identify an individual.

The face is visible to other people in ordinary public life. Yet the automated extraction and comparison of information derived from facial images is different from a passer-by merely noticing another person's appearance.

The technology makes it possible to examine faces systematically and at scale, without requiring the active participation of the people concerned.

That capability may serve legitimate public purposes. It also creates legal questions about the conditions under which the capability should be exercised.

The Dispute Reaches the Court

Bridges brought judicial review proceedings challenging the use of AFR Locate.

Judicial review is a legal procedure through which courts examine the lawfulness of decisions, actions, or failures to act by public authorities. Its central concern is not ordinarily whether a court would have made the same operational choice as the authority. It is whether the authority acted within the powers and legal duties applicable to it.

The case therefore required the courts to consider more than the practical usefulness of facial recognition.

The issues included the right to respect for private life under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, obligations arising under data protection legislation, and the Public Sector Equality Duty imposed by section 149 of the Equality Act 2010.

The Divisional Court dismissed Bridges's claim in September 2019. He appealed.

On 11 August 2020, the Court of Appeal delivered its judgment in R (Bridges) v Chief Constable of South Wales Police.

The appeal succeeded on three grounds and failed on two.

The Court of Appeal concluded that the legal framework governing the use of AFR Locate did not provide sufficiently clear constraints on important aspects of the police's discretion. In particular, the arrangements left too much latitude concerning who could be included on a watchlist and where the technology could be deployed.

The court therefore held that the interference with Article 8 rights arising from the challenged use of the system was not in accordance with the law.

It also found that the police's data protection impact assessment was deficient in the respects identified in the judgment.

A data protection impact assessment is a structured assessment used to identify and evaluate risks associated with processing personal data and to consider measures for addressing those risks. Its significance lies not merely in the existence of a document, but in whether the assessment properly examines the processing and the relevant risks.

The Court of Appeal further held that South Wales Police had failed to comply with the Public Sector Equality Duty. The police had not taken the reasonable steps required to investigate whether the facial recognition software might operate with differential accuracy in relation to race or sex.

This last finding requires particular care.

The court did not determine that AFR Locate had, in fact, discriminated against a particular demographic group. The failure concerned the steps taken to investigate the possibility of such differences in performance.

The distinction is fundamental: failing to investigate a legally relevant risk is not the same factual finding as proving that the risk has materialised.

Nor did the judgment establish that every use of automated facial recognition by police forces is necessarily unlawful.

The Court of Appeal's conclusions concerned the legal framework, assessments, and duties applicable to the deployments and continuing use before it. Its judgment must be read within those limits.

The Question Beneath the Judgment

It would be tempting to describe Bridges as a case about whether facial recognition technology works.

But that would miss much of its significance.

The litigation did not turn simply on whether a computer could compare faces accurately. It required the court to examine how the police selected people for comparison, how the technology was deployed, what information it processed, what safeguards applied, and whether the authority had fulfilled its legal obligations.

In other words, the legal question could not be separated from the institutional setting in which the technology operated.

A system may perform its intended computational task and still be used under inadequate legal safeguards. Equally, the existence of a technical error does not automatically establish every element of a legal claim.

The relationship between technical performance and legal responsibility must be examined, not assumed.

This is the deeper lesson of the case.

When technology becomes part of an institution's decision-making process, the relevant legal inquiry may begin before the final decision is made. It may begin with the choice of data, the selection of individuals for scrutiny, the configuration of the system, the allocation of human responsibilities, or the failure to investigate a foreseeable risk.

The lawyer must therefore learn to ask questions that are both legally meaningful and technically informed.

What, precisely, does the system do?

What information does it use?

What does its output mean?

How is that output transformed into institutional action?

What evidence would allow its operation to be examined?

And which legal duties make those facts relevant?

These questions do not require every lawyer to become a software engineer. They do, however, require a level of technical understanding sufficient to distinguish a system's actual capabilities from claims made about it.

That understanding is what this chapter begins to develop.

Before we can determine when an artificial intelligence system becomes a legal concern, we must understand what we mean by a system, what makes a process automated, and how a computer can produce a result that an institution may later treat as a reason for action.

We begin with those foundations.

CHAPTER ONE

1.2 What Is an AI System?

Before the Algorithm, There Is a Computer

The dispute in Bridges began with a question about the lawful use of facial recognition. Yet beneath that legal question lies a more fundamental one: what, exactly, was the technology doing?

The answer may appear obvious. A camera captured faces, and a computer attempted to recognise them.

But this explanation conceals several important distinctions. A camera is not an algorithm. An algorithm is not necessarily artificial intelligence. A model is not the same thing as a complete technological system. And the result produced by software is not necessarily the decision made by the institution using it.

These distinctions may initially seem technical. They become legally important when we need to identify which part of a process generated an error, who controlled that part, what information was used, and which evidence could establish what actually happened.

To understand them, we must begin with the most familiar object in the chain: the computer.

A computer is an electronic machine that processes information according to instructions. It can receive information, perform operations on it, store information, and produce results.

Consider a calculator.

When a person enters two numbers and requests their addition, the calculator does not need to understand the social meaning of those numbers. They might represent money, years, or the number of people attending an event. The calculator performs a defined mathematical operation and returns a result.

A modern computer can perform vastly more complicated operations. It can display a document, analyse a medical image, search a database, process an application, or help operate a vehicle.

But the basic principle remains: information enters a computational process, operations are performed, and results become available for further use.

This is a useful starting point, although it is not a complete description of every computer system. Computers can interact continuously with their surroundings, communicate with other devices, and adjust their behaviour through software. Nevertheless, understanding information and instructions provides the foundation for understanding more complex systems.

The next question is what the computer actually receives.

Data: How the World Enters a Computer

Imagine that a lawyer receives a handwritten letter.

The lawyer sees words arranged on a page. Those words may describe a contractual dispute, identify the parties, and request legal assistance. The lawyer can interpret the language because they understand its vocabulary, grammar, and context.

A computer does not encounter that letter in precisely the same way.

If the letter is scanned, the computer initially receives a digital representation of the page. The representation consists of information encoded in a form that electronic systems can store and process.

This brings us to the concept of data.

Data are representations of information that can be recorded, stored, communicated, or processed. They may represent numbers, words, images, sounds, dates, locations, or other observations.

A date entered into an application is data. So is the amount of a bank transfer. A photograph stored on a telephone is data, as is a record showing when a document was opened.

The word does not mean that the information is necessarily correct, complete, or meaningful in isolation.

A database may contain an incorrect address. A form may contain an outdated salary. A photograph may be blurred. A medical record may omit relevant information.

The fact that information has been converted into digital form does not establish its reliability.

This distinction matters because computer systems process the information made available to them. If that information is inaccurate, incomplete, or unsuitable for the intended purpose, the results may be affected.

To see why, consider a simple administrative example.

A university uses a computer system to determine whether students satisfy a minimum attendance requirement. The system receives the recorded number of attended classes and the total number of scheduled classes.

If the attendance record is correct, the calculation may accurately reflect the recorded attendance rate.

But suppose several attended classes were never entered into the database. The calculation may still be mathematically correct while the resulting assessment of the student's attendance is factually misleading.

The computer has not necessarily made an arithmetic mistake. The problem lies in the information supplied to it.

This gives us our first important distinction:

A correctly executed computation does not guarantee a correct conclusion about the world.

That principle will become increasingly important as we examine systems that classify individuals, estimate risks, or recommend decisions.

How Computers Represent Information

Computers operate using physical electronic components. At the lowest practical level of ordinary digital representation, information is encoded through patterns of binary digits, commonly called bits.

A bit can take one of two values: 0 or 1.

These values should not be confused with the meaning of the information being represented. A sequence of bits might encode a number, a letter, part of a photograph, or an instruction. Its interpretation depends on the representation system being used.

For example, the letter A can be represented by the number 65 under a familiar character-encoding convention, and that number can itself be expressed in binary.

The important point is not to memorise the binary representation. It is to understand that computers can process text because characters are represented through numerical codes.

Images can also be represented numerically.

A digital image is commonly organised as a grid of small picture elements known as pixels. Each pixel stores values that describe visual properties, such as colour or brightness.

When a person looks at a photograph, they see a face, a building, or a street. A computer can access the numerical values from which that photograph is constructed.

This does not mean that the computer automatically understands what the photograph depicts.

Recognising that a particular arrangement of pixels represents a human face requires additional computational methods.

Those methods are especially important to our discussion of facial recognition. We will examine them in detail in the next section.

For now, we need only understand the foundation: information from the physical world can be represented digitally and made available for computational processing.

Instructions: Telling the Computer What to Do

Data alone do not tell a computer what operations to perform.

A university database may contain attendance records for thousands of students. The computer still needs instructions specifying what to calculate, what conditions to examine, and how to present the results.

These instructions are expressed through software.

Software consists of programs and related components that direct computer operations. A computer program is a set of instructions written in a form that can ultimately be executed by a computer.

Programmers create these instructions using programming languages. Such languages provide structured ways to express operations, conditions, calculations, and interactions with data.

Consider a simple rule:

If a student's recorded attendance is below 75 per cent, display a warning.

A programmer could express that rule through instructions that retrieve the attendance figure, compare it with the threshold, and display the appropriate message.

The computer does not independently decide that 75 per cent is the correct institutional requirement. That value is supplied through the design or configuration of the system.

Nor does the warning necessarily establish that the student has violated a legally enforceable obligation. That conclusion would depend on the applicable rules, the accuracy of the records, and the circumstances of the case.

Here, a familiar legal distinction begins to emerge.

The program performs an operation. The institution determines the rule to be implemented and the consequences attached to its result.

A computer may calculate a condition correctly while the condition itself is inappropriate, incorrectly configured, or applied outside its proper scope.

The law may therefore need to examine both the computational operation and the human decisions surrounding it.

What Is an Algorithm?

The term algorithm is frequently used as though it describes a mysterious form of artificial intelligence.

In computer science, however, the concept is much broader.

An algorithm is a precisely described procedure for carrying out a task or solving a problem through a sequence of steps.

Not every algorithm involves artificial intelligence. Not every algorithm requires a computer.

A recipe provides a useful analogy. It identifies ingredients and specifies operations to be performed in a particular order. A person following the recipe may obtain a predictable result.

The analogy has limits: ordinary recipes may contain vague instructions, whereas computational algorithms require sufficiently precise operations. Nevertheless, the comparison helps explain the underlying idea of an organised procedure.

Consider a more exact example.

Suppose we want to determine which of five numbers is the largest.

We could begin with the first number, compare it with the second, retain the larger value, and continue comparing the retained value with each remaining number.

At the end, the retained value is the largest of the five.

That is an algorithmic method.

It does not learn from experience, recognise patterns in photographs, or generate predictions about human behaviour. It follows a defined procedure.

Algorithms can be used to sort documents, calculate interest, identify duplicate records, or determine the shortest route between locations.

They are fundamental to computing, but they are not synonymous with artificial intelligence.

The distinction between an algorithm and a program is also worth preserving.

An algorithm describes the method. A program expresses operations in executable form, often implementing one or more algorithms alongside other instructions.

Different programs may implement the same algorithm. A single program may use several algorithms.

In practice, these terms are sometimes used loosely, particularly outside technical writing. But legal analysis benefits from greater precision.

If a dispute concerns an incorrect calculation, we may need to determine whether the problem arose from the algorithmic method, its implementation in software, the data supplied to it, or the institutional rule that the program was instructed to apply.

Those are different possibilities. They may require different evidence.

Automation Is Not Necessarily Artificial Intelligence

We are now ready to distinguish two ideas that are often confused: automation and artificial intelligence.

Automation occurs when a process is carried out by a machine or software with reduced need for direct human intervention.

An automatic door provides a simple example. A sensor detects a condition, and the mechanism opens the door according to its programmed operation.

A payroll system may automatically calculate salaries and deductions using predefined formulas.

A university system may automatically send a notification when a recorded deadline has passed.

These systems can be automated without necessarily employing artificial intelligence.

Artificial intelligence is a broader field concerned with developing computational systems capable of performing functions associated with aspects of intelligent behaviour, including reasoning, planning, learning, perception, and language processing.

The field contains several approaches. Some rely on explicit knowledge and logical rules. Others use statistical methods to identify patterns in data. Modern machine learning is especially important, but it does not exhaust the field.

This is why defining artificial intelligence simply as software that learns from data would be too narrow.

It would exclude important approaches to AI that do not depend on learning from examples in the same way as contemporary machine-learning models.

Likewise, defining AI as any software that performs a task automatically would be too broad.

A conventional calculator and a sophisticated facial recognition system both process information. That shared characteristic does not make them technologically equivalent.

The distinction becomes clearer when we consider how systems arrive at their outputs.

Rules, Patterns, and Inference

Imagine two systems designed to assist a bank with loan applications.

The first uses a fixed rule: reject an application if a required document is missing.

The second analyses information about an applicant and produces a score estimating the risk associated with lending to that person.

The first system applies an explicitly specified condition. The second may use a statistical model developed from data to produce an estimate.

Both systems can be automated. Their methods, however, differ.

In the first system, the relevant rule may be directly visible in the program or its configuration.

In the second, the relationship between the inputs and the output may depend on a learned mathematical structure.

This introduces the concept of inference.

In ordinary reasoning, an inference is a conclusion drawn from information. In AI, the term is used in several related technical contexts. In machine learning, inference commonly refers to using an already developed model to produce an output from new input data.

Suppose a model has been trained to distinguish photographs containing cats from photographs that do not contain cats.

When a new photograph is supplied, the model processes its numerical representation and produces a classification or a score.

That operation is commonly called inference.

The model does not need to be retrained every time it receives a photograph. It uses the relationships represented within its existing parameters to process the new input.

This is not the same as proving that the classification is correct. The output remains dependent on the model's design, the data used in its development, and the characteristics of the new photograph.

The difference between producing a result and establishing its reliability is central to our subject.

What Is a Model?

A model is a representation used to describe, approximate, or perform a task involving aspects of the world.

Models exist outside artificial intelligence. Economists use models to study markets. Scientists use models to describe physical processes. Statisticians use models to examine relationships between variables.

In machine learning, a model is a computational structure whose behaviour is shaped, at least in part, through a process of learning from data.

A simple example is a mathematical relationship used to estimate the price of a house from characteristics such as its size and location.

The model may assign different numerical importance to different characteristics. During training, these numerical values can be adjusted using examples of houses with known prices.

The resulting model can then receive information about another house and produce an estimated price.

The estimate may be useful. It is not a guarantee of the price at which the house will actually sell.

A model is therefore not a repository of certainty. It is a structured method for producing outputs under particular assumptions and conditions.

Different models may perform the same task using different mathematical approaches.

Some models are comparatively simple and interpretable. Others involve large numbers of adjustable parameters and complicated relationships that are difficult to describe in ordinary language.

A parameter is a value within a model that influences how it processes information. In many machine-learning approaches, parameters are adjusted during training.

We will study training, parameters, and the differences between machine-learning methods in Chapter Two. At this stage, the important distinction is between a model and the larger system in which it operates.

A model can produce a score without deciding what an institution should do with that score.

That further decision may depend on rules, thresholds, human judgement, and institutional procedures.

A Model Is Not the Whole System

Return to the facial recognition technology used by South Wales Police.

It would be misleading to imagine that a single algorithm simply looked at a person and decided whether the police should intervene.

The operation involved cameras, image processing, facial comparison, watchlists, alert generation, human review, and potential police action.

The facial comparison model was only one component within a larger arrangement.

This larger arrangement is what we mean by a system.

In engineering, a system consists of components organised to perform one or more functions. The components may include hardware, software, data, communication processes, and human operators.

Hardware refers to the physical equipment involved, such as cameras, processors, storage devices, and network equipment.

Software supplies computational instructions and functionality.

Data provide information for processing.

Human operators may configure the technology, review its outputs, correct errors, or decide whether further action is appropriate.

Institutional procedures determine how these activities are organised and governed.

The complete system may therefore have properties that cannot be understood by examining one component in isolation.

A technically accurate model may be used with an unsuitable database.

A reliable model may be configured with an inappropriate threshold.

An alert may be correctly generated but misunderstood by the person reviewing it.

An institution may follow the model's recommendation without examining whether the recommendation is appropriate for the particular case.

Conversely, effective human review may identify an erroneous output before it affects anyone.

These possibilities illustrate why evaluating a system requires more than measuring a model's computational performance.

The relevant inquiry must consider how the components interact.

Why the Intended Purpose Matters

A system cannot be evaluated properly without understanding what it is intended to do.

Consider a tool designed to identify possible duplicate entries in a customer database.

Its purpose is to help staff locate records that may refer to the same person.

Now imagine that an institution begins using the same tool to determine conclusively whether two people have the same legal identity.

The underlying software may not have changed. The institutional significance of its output has changed considerably.

A tool that is adequate for identifying records requiring human review may not be adequate for making a final identity determination.

This is the distinction between a system's intended purpose and the uses to which its outputs may actually be put.

Intended purpose concerns the function and conditions for which a system is designed or supplied. Actual use concerns how an institution employs it in practice.

The difference may affect the evidence needed to evaluate performance and the legal duties that apply.

A model tested under controlled laboratory conditions may behave differently when used in a crowded street, a hospital, or a public administration office.

A system developed for one population may not perform equally well when used with another.

A score designed to assist a professional may acquire a different significance when the professional treats it as binding.

These are not merely questions about software. They concern the relationship between technology and institutional power.

The Legal Meaning of an AI System

We have so far approached artificial intelligence through computer science and engineering.

Law may require a more specific definition.

A legal definition is not necessarily intended to describe every system that computer scientists might study. It establishes the boundaries within which particular legal rules operate.

For example, Article 3(1) of the European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, defines an AI system by reference to a machine-based system operating with varying degrees of autonomy, potentially adapting after deployment, and inferring from inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions capable of influencing physical or virtual environments.

Several elements deserve attention.

First, the definition concerns a machine-based system, not simply an isolated mathematical formula.

Second, it recognises different degrees of autonomy. An AI system need not operate without any human involvement.

Third, adaptability after deployment is possible, not an indispensable characteristic of every AI system.

Fourth, the outputs may take different forms. An AI system may produce a prediction, generate content, recommend an action, or contribute to a decision.

Finally, the definition directs attention to the system's capacity to influence its environment.

The definition serves a regulatory purpose. It helps determine the scope of legislation, but it should not be mistaken for the only scientifically valid way to describe artificial intelligence.

Different legal instruments may adopt different definitions or use related terms for particular regulatory objectives.

The European Union's definition does not automatically determine the legal classification of every technological system in every jurisdiction.

For legal professionals, the practical lesson is to identify both the technical characteristics of a system and the definition used by the law applicable to the dispute.

An engineering description and a legal classification answer related but distinct questions.

The Difference Between an Output and a Decision

We can now return to the question raised by the facial recognition case.

Suppose a system compares a face captured by a camera with photographs stored in a database and generates an alert.

What has happened?

The system has processed information and produced an output indicating a possible match.

What has not necessarily happened?

No final legal determination of identity has necessarily been made. No conclusion about criminal guilt follows from the alert. No police power has necessarily been exercised.

Those further events depend on additional procedures and decisions.

The same distinction appears in other contexts.

A credit-scoring model may produce a numerical assessment of lending risk. A bank may then decide whether to approve an application.

A medical system may identify an image as requiring further examination. A clinician may decide what diagnostic or treatment steps are appropriate.

A recruitment system may rank applications. An employer may decide which candidates to interview.

The relationship between the output and the final decision varies considerably.

Sometimes the output is only one source of information. Sometimes it has substantial practical influence. In other circumstances, an institution may follow it automatically.

The legal consequences cannot be determined from the word recommendation or score alone.

We must examine how the output is used.

This is especially important when human participation is presented as a safeguard.

The mere presence of a human reviewer does not establish that meaningful independent judgement occurred.

A reviewer may have sufficient information, time, authority, and expertise to challenge the system. Alternatively, the reviewer may routinely accept its outputs without effective scrutiny.

Determining which situation exists requires evidence about the real decision-making process.

The System We Must Learn to Examine

We can now identify the basic components of the inquiry that will guide this book.

When confronted with an AI-assisted decision, the legal professional should not begin by asking only whether the algorithm is accurate.

The inquiry should begin by reconstructing the process.

What information enters the system?

Which operations are performed on that information?

Is the output produced by explicit rules, a learned model, or a combination of methods?

What does the output represent?

Who receives it?

What authority does that person or institution have?

How does the output affect the action ultimately taken?

And what evidence is available to examine each stage?

These questions provide a bridge between technical understanding and legal analysis.

They do not replace the legal standards governing privacy, equality, procedural fairness, liability, or evidence. Rather, they help identify the facts to which those standards may apply.

The distinction is important.

Lawyers do not need to know every mathematical operation performed by every AI model. But they must understand enough about the relevant system to recognise when a technical assertion is incomplete, when a result is being given more significance than it can support, and when further expert examination is required.

In Bridges, the legal challenge concerned much more than a computer's capacity to compare facial images.

It concerned a public authority's use of technology to process information about people in public spaces, the choices governing that use, and the legal duties attached to those choices.

Understanding the system makes those questions visible.

But one essential part of the technology remains unexplained.

How can a machine examine an image of a human face and compare it with another image?

To answer that question, we must move from the general architecture of computer systems to the numerical representation of images, the identification of visual patterns, and the mechanisms through which facial recognition software produces possible matches.

That is where we turn next.

1.3 How Can a Machine Recognise a Face?

A Face Is Not a Number—Until We Represent It as One

Return for a moment to the street in Cardiff.

A person walks past a police surveillance camera. To another person standing nearby, the face is immediately recognisable as a human face. It may be familiar or unfamiliar. It may belong to a friend, a colleague, or a complete stranger.

But what does the computer receive?

It does not receive a name, a legal identity, or a direct understanding of the person standing before the camera. It receives image data.

This distinction is fundamental. A human observer sees a face as part of a person. A computer vision system processes a numerical representation of visual information and attempts to identify patterns within it.

Computer vision is the field concerned with developing computational methods for extracting useful information from images and video. Its applications extend far beyond facial recognition. Computer vision systems can examine medical scans, detect objects on roads, inspect manufactured products, and analyse satellite imagery.

Facial recognition is one particular application of this broader field.

To understand it, we must follow the transformation of a face from something visible in the physical world into information that software can compare.

We will do so without assuming that the reader knows how a camera, a digital image, or a machine-learning model works.

From Light to a Digital Photograph

When we look at a person, light reflected from that person's face reaches our eyes. Our visual system processes this light and enables us to perceive shapes, colours, depth, and movement.

A digital camera begins with a related physical event.

Light enters the camera through its optical system and reaches an electronic image sensor. The sensor contains many light-sensitive elements that respond to incoming light. The camera's electronics convert the sensor's measurements into digital information.

The result can be stored or processed as an image.

This description simplifies several engineering processes. Real cameras may also perform operations such as exposure adjustment, colour reconstruction, noise reduction, and image compression. These operations influence the information available in the resulting photograph.

For our purposes, the important principle is that a camera does not transfer a physical face into a computer. It measures light and produces a digital representation.

The representation can be useful, but it is not identical to the physical object.

Consider a photograph taken in poor lighting. The person's face has not necessarily changed, but the recorded image may contain less usable detail. A camera positioned far away may produce an image in which the face occupies only a small portion of the picture.

These conditions matter because facial recognition software works with the available image, not with an ideal version of the person's appearance.

Pixels: The Building Blocks of a Digital Image

A digital photograph can be understood as a grid of tiny elements called pixels.

The word pixel comes from picture element. Each pixel stores numerical values representing visual information at a particular location in the image.

Imagine drawing a small picture on squared paper. Each square contains a colour. Viewed closely, the picture looks like a collection of separate squares. Viewed from a distance, the squares appear to form a continuous image.

A digital image uses a related principle, although real image processing involves more detail than this analogy suggests.

A simple grayscale image represents brightness through numerical values. Under a common eight-bit representation, a value of 0 represents black, a value of 255 represents white, and values between them represent different shades of gray.

A colour image commonly uses several numerical values per pixel. One widely used representation is RGB, which describes the red, green, and blue components of a colour.

For example, in a conventional eight-bit-per-channel RGB representation:

(255, 0, 0) represents red.

(0, 255, 0) represents green.

(0, 0, 255) represents blue.

(255, 255, 255) represents white.

These are examples of a particular digital colour representation, not universal rules for every camera or image format.

Now imagine a photograph measuring 1,000 pixels across and 800 pixels vertically.

It contains 800,000 pixel positions.

In a simple RGB representation, each position may have three colour values. The photograph can therefore be represented through a very large collection of numbers.

Yet none of those numbers, considered individually, means eye, nose, mouth, or person.

The meaning emerges from relationships among many values and from the methods used to analyse them.

This leads to the first major computational challenge: how can software distinguish a face from everything else in an image?

Detecting a Face Is Not Recognising a Person

Suppose a photograph shows a crowded railway station.

There are people, advertisements, doors, signs, bags, and reflections. A computer vision system must first determine which regions of the image are likely to contain faces.

This task is called face detection.

Face detection answers a question such as:

Where are the faces in this image?

It does not necessarily answer:

Whose faces are they?

That distinction is easy to overlook because the two operations may happen within the same application.

A mobile telephone may draw a rectangle around a face when its camera is operating. The rectangle indicates that the software has detected a face-like region. It does not, by itself, establish that the device knows the person's identity.

A facial recognition system ordinarily requires further processing.

In a crowded scene, the detection stage may identify several faces. Each detected region can then be isolated or cropped for additional analysis.

A crop is a selected portion of an image. Instead of processing the entire street scene as one undifferentiated picture, the system can focus on the region containing a particular face.

Detection itself is not infallible.

A face may be partially hidden by another person. The camera may capture the face at an unusual angle. The image may be blurred because the person is moving. Lighting conditions may make important features difficult to distinguish.

The software may fail to detect a face that is present. It may also identify a region that does not actually contain a human face.

These are detection-stage errors.

They should not automatically be treated as errors of identity matching, because the tasks are different.

This distinction matters when interpreting performance claims. A system that performs well at comparing high-quality images may still face difficulties when its cameras cannot reliably obtain usable facial images in the intended environment.

Preparing the Face for Comparison

After detecting a face, a system may perform additional operations to prepare the image.

These operations can include locating important facial landmarks, adjusting orientation, resizing the image, or normalising aspects of its appearance.

A facial landmark is a point associated with a recognisable location or structure on the face, such as the region around an eye, the nose, or the mouth.

Landmark detection can help software estimate the position and orientation of a face.

Why might this be useful?

Imagine two photographs of the same person.

In the first, the person faces the camera directly. In the second, the person's head is tilted slightly.

The photographs differ in their pixel arrangements even though they depict the same individual.

An alignment procedure may attempt to reduce certain differences caused by position or orientation before later comparison.

This does not mean that every facial recognition system uses the same landmarks or alignment method. Modern systems differ in architecture, and some learn to handle variation through their internal representations rather than relying on identical preprocessing procedures.

The broader point is that an image-processing pipeline may include operations intended to make subsequent analysis more consistent.

Such operations can improve the suitability of images for comparison. They can also introduce errors if the face is incorrectly detected, aligned, or transformed.

The reliability of the final result therefore depends partly on what happens before the comparison itself.

From a Photograph to a Biometric Template

We now reach one of the most important concepts in facial recognition: the biometric template.

A biometric characteristic is a measurable biological or behavioural characteristic used in biometric recognition. Facial appearance is one possible source of biometric information.

A biometric template is a digital representation derived from a biometric sample for use in recognition or comparison.

In facial recognition, software processes an image and produces numerical information representing characteristics useful for distinguishing or comparing faces.

This representation is not necessarily a photograph, nor is it a complete description of everything visible in the photograph.

It is information generated for a particular computational purpose.

Consider an analogy.

A fingerprint examiner may describe distinguishing patterns in a fingerprint. The description is not the finger itself. It is a representation of selected characteristics considered useful for comparison.

A facial recognition template serves a related computational function, although the underlying methods are different.

Older approaches to facial recognition could rely more explicitly on measurements or representations associated with particular facial structures. Contemporary systems often use learned numerical representations generated by machine-learning models.

These representations are frequently called feature vectors or embeddings.

A vector, in this context, is an ordered collection of numbers.

For example, the sequence

(0.21, −0.48, 0.73, 0.15)

is a four-dimensional numerical vector.

The numbers above are entirely illustrative. They are not an actual facial template and do not describe any real person.

A modern facial recognition representation may contain many more values.

The purpose of these values is not ordinarily to assign a simple human-readable meaning to each number. One value should not automatically be interpreted as nose width, another as eye colour, and another as age.

In learned representations, information may be distributed across many numerical dimensions. Their combined relationships help the model distinguish or compare images.

This is a crucial distinction between the intuitive explanation of facial features and the internal operation of many modern recognition models.

We may say, informally, that the system extracts facial features. But this does not establish that it stores a straightforward list of anatomical measurements.

The precise representation depends on the technology used.

In Bridges, the Court of Appeal described the extraction of facial characteristics and their expression as numerical values, producing a biometric template. The judgment does not provide a complete public specification of the model's internal mathematical architecture.

Accordingly, the explanations in this section describe established facial recognition principles. They should not be mistaken for a reconstruction of proprietary implementation details that the judgment does not disclose.

How Does the System Learn Which Features Matter?

A natural question now arises.

Who decides which numerical patterns are useful for recognising a face?

In some computer vision methods, engineers explicitly design rules or feature-extraction procedures. In many modern facial recognition systems, machine-learning techniques are used to develop representations from examples.

During training, a model processes data and adjusts its internal parameters to improve performance on a defined task.

For facial recognition, training may involve learning representations that help distinguish images of different people while recognising relationships among images of the same person.

A simplified training objective might encourage the model to produce representations that are closer together for images of the same individual and farther apart for images of different individuals.

This is a conceptual explanation, not a claim that all facial recognition models use an identical training procedure.

Different architectures and learning objectives exist.

The important distinction is between training and using a trained model.

Training is the process through which model parameters are developed or adjusted.

Once a model has been trained, it can process a new facial image and produce a representation without necessarily learning from that image.

This operational use of the model is often called inference.

The distinction matters because a system may use a model trained by one organisation, supplied by another, and deployed by a third.

The people responsible for training, procurement, configuration, and operational use may therefore be different.

Understanding these roles is essential when determining what information is available, which claims can be verified, and who may be responsible for particular aspects of the system.

We will examine machine-learning training in detail in Chapter Two. Here, our immediate concern is what happens when the trained model receives a face for comparison.

Comparing Two Faces

Suppose the system has generated a numerical representation of a face captured by a camera.

It also has a numerical representation derived from an existing photograph.

How can it determine whether the images may depict the same person?

One common approach is to calculate a measure of similarity or distance between the two representations.

A similarity measure expresses how alike two representations are under a defined mathematical rule.

A distance measure expresses how far apart they are according to a particular mathematical definition.

The two concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable in every system.

For example, two vectors may be compared using the angle between them, their numerical distance, or another model-specific scoring procedure.

The resulting number is meaningful only in relation to the method that produced it.

Consider a deliberately simplified example.

A system compares a newly captured face with three stored representations and produces the following illustrative similarity scores:

Stored photographIllustrative similarity score
Photograph A0.91
Photograph B0.46
Photograph C0.18

Assume that this hypothetical scoring method assigns higher values to greater similarity.

Photograph A receives the highest score.

But what does 0.91 mean?

It does not automatically mean that there is a 91 per cent probability that the two photographs depict the same person.

That interpretation would require evidence that the score had been designed or calibrated to represent such a probability.

A similarity score is a mathematical output. Its scale, distribution, and interpretation depend on the system.

A score can be useful for ranking possible matches without being a direct probability of identity.

This distinction is particularly important when scores are presented to non-technical decision-makers.

A number that appears precise may invite more confidence than its actual evidential meaning supports.

Before relying on a score, we must understand what it measures.

Verification and Identification: Two Different Questions

Facial recognition can be used for different tasks.

One task is commonly called verification, or one-to-one comparison.

Verification asks whether a person corresponds to a particular claimed identity.

For example, a person may present an identity document and provide a live facial image. The system compares that image with the photograph associated with the claimed identity.

The question is:

Does this face correspond to the face associated with this particular identity?

A different task is identification, often involving one-to-many comparison.

Here, the system receives a facial image and searches a collection of enrolled identities to find possible correspondences.

The question is:

Does this face correspond to anyone represented in this collection?

These are not identical tasks.

In one-to-one verification, the system evaluates a particular claimed correspondence.

In one-to-many identification, the system may need to examine many possible candidates.

The size and composition of the reference database can affect the nature of the search and its performance.

The system used in Bridges involved comparison against police watchlists. Its operation was therefore closer to the one-to-many identification setting.

This distinction is also recognised in technical evaluation programmes conducted by the United States National Institute of Standards and Technology, or NIST.

NIST evaluates facial recognition algorithms under specified testing conditions, including one-to-many identification tasks. Its evaluations distinguish different error measures and operational configurations.

Those evaluations are valuable for understanding facial recognition technology generally. They do not, without further evidence, establish how the particular system used in Bridges performed during its deployments.

Why a Database Changes the Question

Imagine looking for a particular person in a small room containing five people.

Now imagine searching for the same person in a stadium containing thousands.

The search problem is different, even if the person being sought has not changed.

A facial recognition system faces a related challenge when comparing an image against a database.

A reference database, sometimes called a gallery in biometric evaluation, contains enrolled images or templates against which a new image is compared.

The new image used for searching may be called a probe.

These terms describe roles within a recognition process.

The gallery contains the reference representations. The probe is the new sample being examined.

In a one-to-many search, the system may calculate comparisons against multiple enrolled identities and return one or more candidates.

A candidate is a possible correspondence identified by the system.

It is not necessarily a confirmed identity.

This distinction matters because the system may return the most similar candidate even when the correct person is absent from the database.

Whether such a candidate should be reported as a match depends on the search design, the use of thresholds, and the relevant operating conditions.

For example, a system designed to produce a shortlist for expert review may return several candidates for examination.

Another system may be configured to generate an alert only when a score exceeds a specified threshold.

These approaches serve different purposes and require different methods of evaluation.

The choice is not purely mathematical. It reflects the intended use of the system and the consequences attached to its outputs.

The Threshold: When a Comparison Becomes an Alert

Suppose a facial recognition system produces a similarity score of 0.82.

Should it generate an alert?

The score alone does not answer that question.

The system needs a decision rule.

A threshold is a predefined value used to determine whether a score satisfies a particular condition.

In a hypothetical system where higher scores indicate greater similarity, the rule might be:

Generate an alert if the similarity score is at least 0.80.

Under that illustrative rule, a score of 0.82 would trigger an alert, while a score of 0.77 would not.

These numbers are invented solely to explain the concept. They are not thresholds reported for AFR Locate.

Changing the threshold can change how the system behaves.

A lower threshold may allow more possible matches to be reported. It may also increase the number of incorrect alerts.

A higher threshold may reduce some incorrect alerts while increasing the risk that genuine correspondences will not be reported.

The relationship is a trade-off.

There is no universal threshold that guarantees perfect identification under every condition.

The appropriate configuration depends on the task, the algorithm, the characteristics of the data, and the consequences of different errors.

A system used to organise photographs for personal convenience does not necessarily require the same operational choices as a system used to support policing.

The consequences of an incorrect match are different.

For this reason, a threshold is not merely a number buried in software.

It is part of the system's operational design.

Its selection may affect who is flagged, who is overlooked, how many alerts require human review, and how much confidence an institution can reasonably place in the results.

We will examine the mathematical evaluation of these trade-offs in greater depth in Section 1.4 and Chapter Five.

What Happens When the System Is Wrong?

At least two broad kinds of error are possible in a recognition task.

The system may indicate a correspondence when the images do not depict the same person.

Or it may fail to identify a correspondence when the images do depict the same person.

In ordinary language, these may be described as a false match and a missed match.

However, biometric testing uses more precise terminology because different tasks require different denominators and error definitions.

For one-to-many identification, NIST distinguishes, among other measures, the False Positive Identification Rate (FPIR) and the False Negative Identification Rate (FNIR).

In a thresholded identification search, FPIR concerns searches in which the person is not enrolled in the gallery but the system nevertheless returns one or more candidates above the threshold.

FNIR concerns searches in which the correct person is enrolled but the system fails to return that person above the threshold.

These definitions matter because an error rate is not simply a count of mistakes.

It is a proportion calculated over a defined set of trials.

To interpret the number, we must know which trials were included, what counted as an error, and under what conditions the tests were conducted.

We must also distinguish the system's comparison result from subsequent human action.

An incorrect automated candidate does not necessarily result in a wrongful arrest.

A human reviewer may reject it.

Conversely, a system's technically correct output does not automatically establish that every subsequent institutional action is lawful.

The technical and legal inquiries remain connected but distinct.

Why Image Quality Matters

A facial recognition model does not operate on the physical person. It operates on an image or a representation derived from that image.

Consequently, the quality and characteristics of the image matter.

Several factors may affect recognition performance.

Lighting can change the visible appearance of a face. Strong shadows may conceal details, while excessive brightness can reduce usable information.

Pose also matters. A frontal image and a profile image present different visual information.

Motion can introduce blur.

Low image resolution may limit the detail available for processing.

Occlusion occurs when part of the face is hidden, perhaps by hair, clothing, another person, or an object.

Aging, facial expression, and changes in appearance can also create differences between images.

These factors do not necessarily defeat recognition in every case. Modern systems may be designed and trained to tolerate considerable variation.

But the existence of such capabilities must be established through appropriate evidence rather than assumed.

A performance claim based on carefully controlled photographs may not describe performance under the conditions of a crowded public event.

This is why technical evaluation must examine the relationship between testing conditions and real-world deployment.

The question is not simply whether a model can recognise faces.

It is whether the relevant system can perform the intended task with adequate reliability under the conditions in which it is actually used.

What Can We Learn from a Similarity Score?

A similarity score can provide evidence that two digital representations are close according to a specified computational method.

It cannot, without further analysis, establish everything a legal decision-maker may wish to know.

It does not necessarily reveal the model's internal reasoning.

It does not automatically identify the probability that a particular person is the subject of the image.

It does not establish why the person was included in a watchlist.

It does not determine whether the original photograph was lawfully obtained.

It does not establish that the camera captured a sufficiently clear image.

And it does not determine whether police intervention is justified.

These are separate questions requiring different kinds of evidence.

Some concern software and statistical performance. Others concern data governance, institutional procedures, human judgement, and legal authority.

The distinction is especially important because a numerical result may appear objective.

Numbers can create an impression of certainty. But the reliability of a numerical result depends on the process through which it was produced and the proposition it is being used to support.

A facial similarity score is not a legal conclusion.

It is one output of a technological process.

Reconstructing the Facial Recognition Process

We can now assemble the stages into a coherent sequence.

First, a camera captures visual information and produces digital image data.

Second, software examines the image to detect regions that appear to contain human faces.

Third, the detected facial regions may be isolated and prepared for further processing.

Fourth, a feature-extraction process produces numerical representations of the faces.

Fifth, the representations are compared with stored reference representations.

Sixth, the system produces similarity scores or candidate results.

Seventh, an operational rule determines whether a result should be reported as a possible match.

Finally, the reported result may be reviewed by a human operator and used, or rejected, within an institutional decision-making process.

This is a general explanatory sequence. Actual systems may combine stages, use different internal architectures, or perform operations in a different order.

The sequence is nevertheless useful because it identifies distinct points at which questions can be asked.

Was the image suitable for analysis?

Did the software detect the correct face?

Was the representation generated reliably?

What reference data were used?

How was similarity calculated?

What threshold or candidate-selection rule applied?

How was the result communicated?

What did the human reviewer actually do?

Each question concerns a different part of the process.

Each may require different evidence.

And each may become legally relevant depending on the circumstances.

Returning to Bridges: What Do We Actually Know?

The Court of Appeal's judgment provides a useful description of AFR Locate's operational stages.

It identifies the use of a watchlist, live facial image acquisition, face detection, feature extraction, face comparison, and matching through similarity scores and thresholds.

It also describes the role of human operators in reviewing possible matches.

These are facts drawn from the judicial record.

But the judgment does not publicly establish every detail that a technical auditor might wish to examine.

For example, the court's general description does not, by itself, provide a complete account of the software's training data, model architecture, internal parameters, or every configuration choice made during deployment.

Nor does the judgment's description of similarity scoring justify treating the score as a calibrated probability of identity.

We must therefore resist two opposite errors.

The first is to assume that the system is technically reliable simply because it produces numerical results.

The second is to assume that the system is unreliable simply because its internal design is not fully described in a court judgment.

Both conclusions would go beyond the available evidence.

A sound assessment requires identifying what is known, what remains uncertain, and what additional evidence would be needed to answer the relevant question.

This is precisely the discipline that legal–technical analysis demands.

The Point at Which Engineering Becomes Legally Significant

We began with a face passing before a camera.

We now understand that the apparent simplicity of facial recognition conceals a sequence of computational operations.

The camera measures light.

The digital image represents visual information numerically.

Software detects facial regions.

A model or other computational method extracts information useful for comparison.

The system compares that information with stored references.

A scoring or candidate-selection procedure produces a result.

An operational rule may turn the result into an alert.

A human operator or institution may then act upon it.

At every stage, technical design choices affect what the system can do and what its outputs can reasonably support.

The legal significance of those choices depends on the rights, duties, powers, and interests engaged by the system's use.

A person may be affected not because a machine has reached a legally authoritative conclusion, but because an institution treats a machine-generated result as a reason for further action.

That is the point at which understanding the technology becomes essential to understanding the law.

We have learned how facial recognition can produce a possible match.

We have not yet established how much confidence should be placed in that match.

To answer that question, we must examine the meaning of similarity scores, the selection of thresholds, the frequency and consequences of errors, and the evidence needed to evaluate performance.

The next section begins with a deceptively simple question:

When a machine says two faces match, how certain should we be?

1.4 When Similarity Becomes an Alert

The Number That Appears to Know

Imagine a police officer standing beside a computer screen.

A surveillance camera has captured the face of someone walking through a public square. The facial recognition system has compared that image with photographs in a police watchlist. Within moments, a notification appears.

The software has identified a possible match.

Alongside the alert is a numerical score.

To someone unfamiliar with the technology, the number may seem reassuring. It suggests that the system has performed a calculation rather than made a subjective judgement. It appears precise, measurable, and potentially more reliable than human memory.

But precision in calculation is not the same as certainty about identity.

The software may have calculated the score exactly as designed. That does not establish that the two photographs depict the same person. Nor does it tell us how often the system makes mistakes, whether the images were suitable for comparison, or whether the score justifies any particular police action.

The number answers a technical question defined by the system's design.

The institution must still determine what that answer means and what, if anything, should follow from it.

This is where the relationship between artificial intelligence and law becomes especially delicate. A numerical output can appear to resolve a question that it has merely helped to investigate.

To understand the problem, we must distinguish four ideas that are frequently confused: similarity, probability, accuracy, and evidential confidence.

They are related, but they are not interchangeable.

What Does a Similarity Score Actually Measure?

In the previous section, we learned that facial recognition software can transform facial images into numerical representations.

A comparison method can then measure a relationship between two such representations.

The result may be called a similarity score.

The score indicates how closely the representations correspond according to the mathematical method used by the system.

This qualification is essential.

A similarity score does not measure resemblance in every possible human sense. It measures a relationship defined by a particular computational procedure.

Consider two photographs of the same individual.

One was taken indoors, with the person looking directly at the camera. The other was captured outdoors, with the person's head turned slightly.

The images may differ considerably in their pixel values. Yet a well-performing recognition model may produce representations that are sufficiently similar to indicate that the images depict the same person.

Now consider photographs of two different individuals with similar facial appearances.

The model may produce representations that are relatively close, even though the individuals are not the same person.

The purpose of the recognition method is to distinguish these situations as reliably as possible.

But the distinction is not always perfect.

The score is therefore best understood as an output of a comparison process, not as a declaration of identity.

A further complication is that scores are not necessarily expressed on a universal scale.

One system might produce scores between zero and one. Another might use a different numerical range. A third might calculate a distance rather than a similarity value.

Even when two systems use the same numerical range, their scores need not have the same statistical meaning.

A score of 0.85 from one system cannot automatically be compared with a score of 0.85 from another.

The score must be interpreted within the system that produced it.

Similarity Is Not Probability

Suppose a facial recognition system produces a similarity score of 0.92.

A person looking at the result might say:

“There is a 92 per cent chance that these photographs show the same person.”

That conclusion does not follow from the score alone.

A similarity score of 0.92 may indicate a high degree of resemblance under the model's comparison method. But it does not necessarily represent a 92 per cent probability of shared identity.

To understand why, we must introduce a basic idea from probability.

Probability expresses uncertainty about an event or proposition under a defined mathematical interpretation. A probability of 0.9 corresponds to 90 per cent.

But a number becomes a meaningful probability only when its interpretation is justified.

For example, a model may be designed to produce scores that rank possible matches. Higher scores may generally correspond to stronger similarity. That does not establish that the scores are calibrated probabilities.

Calibration concerns the relationship between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes.

In a well-calibrated probability model, events assigned a probability of approximately 80 per cent should occur approximately 80 per cent of the time across an appropriate collection of comparable predictions.

Calibration is assessed empirically. It cannot be assumed merely because the output falls between zero and one.

A facial recognition system may be effective at ranking candidate photographs without producing a probability that a particular candidate is the correct person.

This distinction matters because a decision-maker may place inappropriate confidence in a score if its meaning is misunderstood.

A lawyer examining a technical report should therefore ask:

What does the score represent?

How is it calculated?

Has it been calibrated as a probability?

Under what conditions was its interpretation evaluated?

Without answers, the numerical value cannot support every conclusion that might be drawn from it.

The Threshold: Turning a Measurement into a Classification

A facial recognition system may calculate a score without producing an alert.

To decide when an alert should be generated, the system can apply a threshold.

A threshold is a value used to separate results into different operational categories.

Imagine a hypothetical system in which higher scores indicate greater similarity.

Suppose its alert threshold is set at 0.80.

A score of 0.83 generates an alert.

A score of 0.78 does not.

The system has applied a rule.

But notice what has happened.

The original output was a numerical measurement. The threshold converted that measurement into an operational classification: alert or no alert.

The difference between 0.78 and 0.83 may be relatively small. Yet the operational consequences may be substantial.

One person may be brought to an officer's attention while another is not.

The threshold therefore performs a function beyond calculation. It determines how the institution's workflow responds to the model's output.

The precise threshold used by a system may be selected during development, testing, or deployment. It may be influenced by the system's intended purpose and the consequences of different errors.

A threshold is not automatically correct merely because it was chosen by a developer.

Its appropriateness must be evaluated in relation to the task.

This introduces a central engineering principle: changing a threshold usually changes the balance between different kinds of mistakes.

Two Ways a Recognition System Can Be Wrong

Consider a watchlist containing photographs of people whom police are seeking.

A camera captures a person who is not represented on the watchlist.

The system nevertheless generates an alert associating that person with a watchlist entry.

This is an incorrect identification result.

Now consider the opposite situation.

A person whose photograph is on the watchlist passes before the camera, but the system fails to return that person as a qualifying match.

This is a missed identification.

The two errors have different operational consequences.

An incorrect alert may direct police attention towards the wrong individual.

A missed identification may prevent the system from alerting officers to someone it was intended to locate.

Neither outcome can be understood adequately by asking only whether the system is accurate.

We need to know what kinds of errors occur and how frequently they occur.

The terminology requires care.

In general classification problems, we often speak of false positives and false negatives. But biometric identification uses task-specific definitions, particularly when a system searches a database containing many enrolled identities.

The distinction matters because a one-to-one comparison and a one-to-many search do not have identical evaluation structures.

Understanding the Four Basic Outcomes

Before examining the more specialised biometric measures, consider a simple hypothetical decision problem.

A system is asked whether a particular condition is present.

There are two possibilities in reality: the condition is present or absent.

The system also has two possible responses: positive or negative.

Combining them gives four outcomes.

Actual conditionSystem responseOutcome
PresentPositiveTrue positive
AbsentPositiveFalse positive
PresentNegativeFalse negative
AbsentNegativeTrue negative

A true positive occurs when the system correctly reports that the condition is present.

A false positive occurs when it reports the condition as present even though it is absent.

A false negative occurs when it fails to report a condition that is present.

A true negative occurs when it correctly reports that the condition is absent.

These terms describe the relationship between the system's response and the actual condition.

They do not, by themselves, establish the legal significance of the result.

For example, an incorrect alert in a photograph-organising application may be a minor inconvenience.

An incorrect alert in a policing context may result in additional scrutiny of a person.

The technical classification of an error and its legal consequences are distinct questions.

The Confusion Matrix: Making Errors Visible

A confusion matrix is a table that records how often a classification system produces each of the four outcomes.

Despite its name, it is not intended to confuse the reader. It helps reveal patterns that a single accuracy figure can conceal.

Imagine a hypothetical system evaluated on 1,000 independent cases.

In 100 cases, the condition being tested is genuinely present.

In the remaining 900 cases, it is absent.

Suppose the system produces the following results:

Actually presentActually absent
System reports positive9045
System reports negative10855

The system correctly identifies 90 of the 100 positive cases.

It misses 10.

Among the 900 negative cases, it correctly rejects 855 but incorrectly flags 45.

The total number of correct results is:

90 + 855 = 945.

The total number of cases is 1,000.

Its overall accuracy is therefore:

[ \text{Accuracy}=\frac{945}{1000}=94.5%. ]

At first glance, 94.5 per cent may sound impressive.

But the figure does not tell us how the errors are distributed.

To understand the system properly, we need additional measures.

This numerical example is hypothetical and is intended to explain classification evaluation. It is not a measurement of the system used by South Wales Police, and its binary classification structure should not be treated as a substitute for the specialised evaluation of one-to-many biometric identification.

Sensitivity: How Often Does the System Find What It Is Looking For?

One important measure is sensitivity, also called the true positive rate or recall in relevant classification settings.

Sensitivity asks:

Among the cases in which the condition is actually present, how many does the system correctly identify?

The calculation is:

[ \text{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}. ]

Here, TP means true positives and FN means false negatives.

Using our hypothetical results:

[ \text{Sensitivity}=\frac{90}{90+10}=90%. ]

The system correctly identifies 90 per cent of the genuinely positive cases.

This also means that it misses 10 per cent of them.

The false negative rate is therefore:

[ \text{FNR}=\frac{FN}{TP+FN}=10%. ]

These percentages are calculated over the cases in which the condition is genuinely present.

That denominator is essential.

A rate cannot be interpreted correctly unless we know which cases were counted.

Specificity: How Often Does the System Correctly Reject Negative Cases?

Another important measure is specificity, also called the true negative rate.

Specificity asks:

Among the cases in which the condition is actually absent, how many does the system correctly classify as negative?

Its formula is:

[ \text{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}. ]

Using our example:

[ \text{Specificity}=\frac{855}{855+45}=95%. ]

The system correctly rejects 95 per cent of the genuinely negative cases.

The remaining 5 per cent are incorrectly classified as positive.

This is the false positive rate:

[ \text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}=5%. ]

The false positive rate is not the same as the proportion of positive alerts that are incorrect.

That distinction is so important that it deserves separate examination.

When a Positive Alert Is Wrong

Return to the hypothetical confusion matrix.

The system generated 135 positive results:

90 true positives and 45 false positives.

Of those 135 positive results, 90 were correct.

The proportion of positive results that were correct is called precision, or positive predictive value in the appropriate setting.

[ \text{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}. ]

In our example:

[ \text{Precision}=\frac{90}{90+45}\approx66.7%. ]

Although the system's overall accuracy is 94.5 per cent, only about two-thirds of its positive alerts are correct.

The remaining one-third are incorrect.

This is not a mathematical contradiction.

Accuracy and precision answer different questions.

Accuracy asks how often the system produces the correct classification across all cases.

Precision asks how often a positive result is correct.

The difference matters when the practical consequences of the system are concentrated among the people receiving positive results.

A system may classify most cases correctly while generating a significant proportion of incorrect alerts.

A lawyer examining an automated system should therefore resist claims based solely on overall accuracy.

The relevant performance measure depends on the question being asked and the consequences of the system's outputs.

The Base-Rate Problem: Why Rare Events Change the Meaning of Alerts

There is another complication.

The reliability of a positive alert can depend on how common the condition being detected is within the population being examined.

This is known as the base-rate problem.

A base rate is the frequency with which a condition occurs within a relevant population.

Suppose a system is used to identify a relatively rare condition.

Even a system with high sensitivity and specificity may produce many false alerts if the condition is sufficiently uncommon.

Consider a new hypothetical example.

Imagine a population of 10,000 people.

Assume that 100 people genuinely satisfy the condition being sought, while 9,900 do not.

The condition is therefore present in 1 per cent of the population.

Suppose a hypothetical classifier has:

Sensitivity of 90 per cent.

Specificity of 99 per cent.

Among the 100 genuinely positive cases, the system correctly flags 90 and misses 10.

Among the 9,900 genuinely negative cases, it incorrectly flags 1 per cent:

[ 9,900\times0.01=99. ]

The system therefore generates 189 positive alerts:

90 correct alerts and 99 incorrect alerts.

Its precision is:

[ \frac{90}{189}\approx47.6%. ]

Fewer than half of its positive alerts are correct, even though the system has 90 per cent sensitivity and 99 per cent specificity.

The reason is not that the arithmetic has failed.

The reason is that the condition is rare, and the large number of negative cases creates many opportunities for false positives.

This is one of the most important lessons in statistical reasoning.

A small error rate applied to a very large population can produce a substantial number of errors.

The example is deliberately simplified. Its numerical assumptions are hypothetical, and its binary classification structure is not a direct model of the specialised one-to-many biometric evaluation used by NIST.

Nevertheless, it illustrates a general statistical principle that matters when interpreting alerts generated during large-scale screening.

Why the Base Rate Matters to Legal Evidence

Suppose a legal decision-maker is told that a system has a false positive rate of only 1 per cent.

The statement may sound reassuring.

But it is incomplete.

One per cent of what?

Was the rate measured across individual comparisons, people searched, entire identification transactions, or some other unit?

How many searches were performed?

How frequently was the target condition genuinely present?

Were the test conditions comparable to the operational setting?

These questions are not minor technical details.

They determine what the number can establish.

In a legal dispute, an expert may provide a scientifically accurate statement about an error rate. But if the statement is misunderstood, it may be used to support a conclusion that does not follow.

For example, a false positive rate of 1 per cent does not mean that every positive result has a 99 per cent probability of being correct.

That would confuse the probability of a positive result when the condition is absent with the probability that the condition is present given a positive result.

The two probabilities are different.

Bayes' theorem provides a mathematical framework for relating them.

In a simplified binary classification setting, the positive predictive value can be expressed as:

[ P(C\mid +)= \frac{P(+\mid C)P(C)} {P(+\mid C)P(C)+P(+\mid \neg C)P(\neg C)}. ]

Here, (C) means the condition is genuinely present, and (+) means the system returns a positive result.

The formula tells us that the meaning of a positive result depends not only on the system's sensitivity and false positive rate, but also on how common the condition is.

The legal professional does not need to perform every calculation personally.

But they should understand enough to recognise when an expert's statement, a vendor's performance claim, or an institutional explanation omits a necessary denominator or base-rate assumption.

One-to-Many Identification Requires Different Measures

The preceding examples use a simple binary classification framework.

Facial identification against a watchlist is more complicated.

A single image may be compared against hundreds or thousands of enrolled identities.

The system may return several candidates, one candidate, or no candidate.

The correct person may be present in the database or absent from it.

These possibilities require performance measures specifically designed for identification searches.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology distinguishes two important measures in thresholded one-to-many identification.

The first is the False Positive Identification Rate, abbreviated FPIR.

In this setting, FPIR is the proportion of searches in which the person is not enrolled in the gallery but the system returns one or more candidates above the specified threshold.

The second is the False Negative Identification Rate, abbreviated FNIR.

In a thresholded identification setting, FNIR is the proportion of searches in which the correct person is enrolled but is not returned above the threshold.

These definitions differ from the false positive and false negative rates of an ordinary binary classifier.

Their denominators are searches, not necessarily individual pairwise comparisons.

The distinction is essential.

Suppose a search image is compared with 1,000 enrolled photographs.

The system may perform numerous internal comparisons, but the operational event being evaluated may be whether the entire search returns an incorrect candidate.

Counting comparison-level errors and counting search-level errors are not the same exercise.

A report that describes one kind of error rate cannot automatically be interpreted as reporting the other.

NIST also evaluates identification systems configured to return ranked candidate lists for human investigation. Such systems may be assessed differently from systems that use thresholds to support more automated decisions.

The correct evaluation method depends on the intended application.

The Watchlist Is Part of the Performance Question

A facial recognition system does not search an abstract population.

It searches a particular collection of enrolled images or templates.

That collection matters.

A watchlist may contain photographs of different quality, age, or origin.

Some individuals may be represented by more than one image, depending on the system's design and operational arrangements.

Images may have been taken under different lighting conditions or with different cameras.

The number of enrolled identities may also change.

These characteristics can affect the search problem.

An evaluation performed with a small, carefully controlled gallery may not predict performance in a substantially different operational gallery.

This does not mean that a larger gallery necessarily makes every system unreliable. The relationship depends on the algorithm, the task, the threshold, and the characteristics of the data.

But gallery size and composition are relevant variables that should be documented and considered.

In Bridges, the composition of the watchlist was also legally important for a different reason.

The Court of Appeal examined the breadth of police discretion concerning who could be placed on the watchlist.

That was not merely a question about whether the recognition algorithm could compare photographs correctly.

It concerned the institutional rules governing the people selected for possible identification.

Thus, the watchlist has at least two distinct dimensions.

Technically, it forms part of the reference data against which images are compared.

Legally, its creation and use may engage questions about authority, discretion, necessity, and safeguards.

Both dimensions matter, but they should not be confused.

A Threshold Creates a Trade-Off

Let us return to the threshold.

Suppose a system uses a high threshold before generating an alert.

The system may reject many weak similarities.

This may reduce incorrect alerts, but it may also miss genuine correspondences that produce scores below the threshold.

Now suppose the threshold is lowered.

More results may qualify as possible matches.

Some genuine correspondences previously missed may now be reported.

But more incorrect correspondences may also be reported.

This is the fundamental trade-off between different types of identification error.

The relationship can be studied by evaluating a system across multiple threshold settings.

In many classification and verification settings, engineers use performance curves to examine how error rates change as the threshold varies.

For example, a receiver operating characteristic curve, or ROC curve, plots the true positive rate against the false positive rate across different thresholds in an appropriate binary classification setting.

The curve helps illustrate how the system's behaviour changes when the decision rule changes.

It does not determine which threshold is legally or institutionally appropriate.

That requires consideration of the intended use and the consequences of different errors.

In policing, an incorrect alert may create risks of unnecessary scrutiny or intervention.

A missed identification may affect an investigation or a public safety objective.

The relative importance of those outcomes cannot be decided by mathematics alone.

Technical evaluation can show the expected effects of different configurations under specified assumptions.

Institutional and legal judgement must determine which risks are acceptable and under what safeguards.

Why There Is No Single Meaningful Accuracy Number

A vendor might advertise a facial recognition system as 99 per cent accurate.

What does that mean?

It might refer to the proportion of correct results in a particular benchmark.

It might describe one-to-one verification under specified conditions.

It might describe identification performance for a particular gallery size.

It might refer to a selected threshold, dataset, or image-quality category.

It might even refer to a metric that is unsuitable for the intended deployment.

Without further information, the claim is incomplete.

A scientifically meaningful performance statement should identify the task, the metric, the test data, the operating conditions, and the relevant decision rule.

It should also disclose important limitations.

Consider two systems.

System A performs exceptionally well on high-quality passport-style photographs.

System B performs somewhat less well on those photographs but has been specifically evaluated under variable lighting and camera angles.

If an institution intends to use facial recognition in a crowded outdoor environment, the difference between those testing conditions may be important.

A single headline accuracy figure would not reveal it.

The question is not simply which system has the higher advertised number.

It is which evidence is relevant to the conditions of the proposed use.

Testing Conditions and the Problem of Generalisation

A model may perform well on the data used during its development.

That does not guarantee equivalent performance on new data.

This issue is known as generalisation.

Generalisation concerns how effectively a model performs on examples or conditions beyond those from which its behaviour was learned or tuned.

In machine learning, a distinction is commonly made between training data and evaluation data.

Training data are used to develop the model.

Evaluation data are used to assess how the model performs under defined conditions.

A credible evaluation should account for whether the testing process provides an appropriate measure of performance on data not used to train or tune the model.

If the evaluation is poorly designed, reported performance may be misleading.

For example, repeated appearances of the same underlying examples across development and evaluation datasets can make a model appear more successful than it would be on genuinely independent cases.

This is one form of the broader problem known as data leakage.

Data leakage occurs when information unavailable under the intended prediction conditions improperly influences model development or evaluation.

We will examine data quality and leakage in detail in Chapter Four.

Here, the immediate lesson is that performance numbers must be understood in relation to how they were obtained.

A result is not automatically reliable merely because it appears in a technical report.

The methodology matters.

Performance May Differ Across Populations

Facial recognition performance can vary across different demographic groups.

This is not a claim that every algorithm performs identically poorly for every group, or that every observed difference proves unlawful discrimination.

It is a technical issue requiring careful empirical investigation.

NIST's 2019 report, Face Recognition Vendor Test Part 3: Demographic Effects, examined demographic differences in facial recognition performance across a substantial collection of algorithms.

The report documented differences in error rates associated with demographic characteristics, while also demonstrating that results varied across algorithms, applications, and evaluation conditions.

The relevant lesson is not that a single demographic error rate can be assigned to facial recognition technology as a whole.

Different systems may have different performance characteristics.

Nor does evidence about a group-level error rate establish that a particular individual was incorrectly identified because of membership in that group.

Group-level statistical evidence and case-specific causation are distinct matters.

Nevertheless, demographic performance differences may be important to the assessment of a system's reliability and to legal duties concerning equality and discrimination.

The Bridges litigation illustrates this connection.

The Court of Appeal did not find that the particular AFR Locate system had been proved to discriminate against people on the basis of race or sex.

It found that South Wales Police had failed to take reasonable steps to investigate whether the software might operate with differential accuracy in those respects, as required by the Public Sector Equality Duty.

This was a finding about the discharge of a legal duty to investigate a relevant risk.

It was not a judicial measurement of the system's demographic error rates.

That distinction must remain clear.

A Test Result Is Not an Operational Guarantee

Suppose an independent laboratory evaluates a facial recognition algorithm and reports its performance under specified conditions.

The result may provide valuable evidence.

But it does not automatically establish that every deployment using that algorithm will perform identically.

The deployed system may use different cameras, image-processing components, databases, thresholds, or human review procedures.

The operational environment may differ from the evaluation environment.

Software versions may change.

A system may also be configured differently from the version that was tested.

These differences matter because the performance of a complete system can depend on more than the underlying recognition algorithm.

For this reason, a careful evaluation should distinguish algorithm-level performance from system-level performance.

Algorithm-level testing examines the performance of a particular computational component under defined conditions.

System-level testing examines how the components operate together in the intended environment.

Operational monitoring then examines how the deployed system continues to behave over time.

These forms of evidence are related but not interchangeable.

An independent benchmark may support a claim about an algorithm's performance on a particular test.

It does not, by itself, prove that a police deployment complied with legal requirements or that every alert generated during the deployment was correct.

The Human Reviewer: A Safeguard or Another Source of Error?

The facial recognition process does not necessarily end when an alert is generated.

In the arrangement described in Bridges, a police operator reviewed the possible match before further action could follow.

Human review can provide an important safeguard.

An operator may recognise that two images depict different people despite a high similarity score.

The operator may also consider contextual information unavailable to the recognition model.

But the presence of a human reviewer does not automatically eliminate error.

Humans can make mistakes.

A reviewer may be influenced by the apparent authority of a computer-generated result.

This possibility is often discussed under the concept of automation bias: the tendency, in relevant circumstances, to rely excessively on automated advice or outputs.

The extent of such reliance depends on the task, the interface, the reviewer, and the institutional environment. It should not be assumed in every case.

A reviewer may also face time pressure, limited information, poor image quality, or inadequate training.

Consequently, the effectiveness of human oversight must be assessed rather than presumed.

A meaningful evaluation may ask whether reviewers can reject alerts, whether they understand the system's limitations, whether their decisions are recorded, and whether the institution examines disagreements between human reviewers and automated outputs.

The technical performance of the model and the effectiveness of human review are separate dimensions of system reliability.

Four Different Errors in One Operational Process

Consider a hypothetical facial recognition deployment.

A camera captures a person walking through a public square.

Several different failures are possible.

First, the camera or detection software may fail to obtain a usable facial image.

Second, the recognition component may generate an incorrect candidate or fail to identify the correct one.

Third, a human reviewer may incorrectly accept or reject the candidate.

Fourth, an officer may take an inappropriate action after receiving the reviewed alert.

These failures occur at different stages.

They should not be combined into a single undifferentiated concept of algorithmic error.

A recognition algorithm could produce a correct result while the subsequent human decision is mistaken.

Alternatively, the algorithm could produce an incorrect candidate that the human reviewer successfully rejects.

The final operational outcome depends on the sequence of events.

This is why a system-level audit should trace the pathway from input to action.

It should identify where an error occurred and what evidence supports that conclusion.

Without such reconstruction, responsibility may be attributed to the wrong component or person.

What Evidence Would Allow Us to Evaluate an Alert?

Imagine that a lawyer is asked to examine a disputed facial recognition alert.

The first question should not necessarily be whether the algorithm is biased.

That may become relevant, but the inquiry must begin by identifying the disputed event.

What image was captured?

What reference image or template was used?

What software and configuration were operating?

What score was produced?

What threshold applied?

What result was displayed?

Who reviewed it?

What action followed?

These questions lead to different categories of potential evidence.

The original image may help assess image quality and whether the face was correctly detected.

The reference image and watchlist records may help establish which identity the system was attempting to match.

Technical documentation may explain the scoring method and threshold configuration.

Evaluation reports may provide information about expected performance under specified conditions.

System logs may record the time of an alert, the software version, the candidate returned, or the actions taken by an operator, depending on the system's logging arrangements.

Human review records may show whether the candidate was accepted, rejected, or escalated.

Institutional policies may explain what officers were authorised to do with an alert.

Not every system retains all these records. Nor is every category of information necessarily available or lawfully disclosable in every proceeding.

The evidential task is therefore to identify what exists, what is relevant, what can lawfully be obtained, and what limitations follow from missing information.

A technical explanation is not a substitute for evidence about the actual event.

The Limits of a Performance Report

Suppose a manufacturer provides a report showing that its algorithm achieved a low false positive identification rate in an independent evaluation.

What can the report establish?

Subject to its methodology and authenticity, it may provide evidence about the algorithm's performance under the conditions of that evaluation.

What can it not automatically establish?

It cannot prove that a particular alert was correct.

It cannot establish that the deployed system used the same software version or configuration.

It cannot establish that the watchlist was lawfully compiled.

It cannot prove that an officer conducted meaningful review.

It cannot determine whether an intervention was proportionate or otherwise lawful.

Those conclusions require additional facts and legal analysis.

This distinction is not an argument against technical benchmarking.

Independent evaluation is valuable precisely because it provides structured evidence about particular aspects of performance.

The problem arises when a valid result is used to support a proposition beyond its evidential scope.

A lawyer must therefore ask not only whether a source is authoritative, but also what proposition the source actually supports.

Returning to the Cardiff Deployments

The Bridges judgment recorded ten possible matches during the Queen Street deployment on 21 December 2017.

Eight were described as true matches and two as false matches.

It also recorded one true match and no false alerts during the Motorpoint Arena deployment on 27 March 2018.

These are important operational facts.

But they do not, by themselves, permit calculation of every meaningful facial recognition performance metric.

To calculate a reliable false positive identification rate, for example, we would need the relevant number of non-mated searches and a clear definition of the search outcomes being counted.

To calculate a false negative identification rate, we would need information about searches in which the correct identity was present in the gallery, including cases where the system failed to return that identity.

A count of alerts does not necessarily reveal the number of missed identifications.

Nor can the proportion of false alerts among reported alerts automatically be treated as the false positive identification rate.

The denominators are different.

It would therefore be incorrect to take the two false matches among ten reported possible matches and conclude that AFR Locate had a 20 per cent false positive identification rate.

That calculation would answer a different, narrower question about the composition of the reported matches for that deployment, assuming the classifications were established as recorded.

It would not provide the NIST-defined FPIR.

This example illustrates why technical terminology matters in legal writing.

Two percentages may look similar while measuring different events.

A court, regulator, or legal adviser must know which event the number describes.

From Measurement to Legal Significance

We can now return to the person standing before the camera.

The facial recognition system may calculate a similarity score.

A threshold may convert that score into an alert.

A reviewer may accept the alert.

An officer may then decide whether to approach the person.

At each stage, information is interpreted and transformed.

The similarity score is a computational result.

The alert is an operational classification.

The review is a human assessment.

The intervention is an institutional action.

These are not the same thing.

The reliability of each stage must be examined using evidence appropriate to that stage.

The legal significance of the process depends on the rights, duties, and powers involved.

A mistaken alert may be relevant to the assessment of reliability, but it does not automatically prove every element of a legal claim.

A correct alert may support identification, but it does not automatically justify every subsequent action.

A technically sophisticated system may be deployed under inadequate governance arrangements.

And a system with strong benchmark performance may still require careful examination of its real-world use.

The central lesson is that technical accuracy is not a single property that can be asserted without qualification.

It is a collection of measurable claims about particular tasks, conditions, and outcomes.

The Question a Lawyer Must Learn to Ask

When someone says that an AI system is 99 per cent accurate, the most useful immediate response is not admiration or suspicion.

It is a request for clarification.

Accurate at what?

Measured against which reference?

Using which dataset?

Under what conditions?

At what threshold?

With which denominator?

For which population?

And how does that result relate to the decision the institution intends to make?

These questions are not attempts to turn lawyers into statisticians.

They are necessary steps in determining whether a technical assertion supports a legally relevant proposition.

The purpose of technical literacy is not to replace expert evidence. It is to make legal professionals capable of identifying what expert evidence is needed, understanding its limits, and recognising when conclusions exceed the information available.

The facial recognition system in Bridges did not simply transform faces into legal decisions.

It produced outputs within a larger institutional process.

To understand the lawfulness of that process, we must examine not only whether the technology could identify possible matches, but also how the police used those outputs, who controlled the relevant choices, and what safeguards governed the resulting actions.

We have followed the transformation from image to numerical representation, from representation to similarity score, and from score to alert.

The next question concerns what happens after the alert leaves the computer.

When an automated system identifies a person, who actually makes the decision—and where does responsibility lie?

CHAPTER ONE

1.5 From Alert to Institutional Action

The Moment the Machine Hands Over

A facial recognition system has generated an alert.

On a screen, two images appear beside one another. One comes from a surveillance camera. The other belongs to a person represented on a police watchlist. The software has calculated that the images are sufficiently similar to justify drawing them to an operator's attention.

At this moment, something important changes.

Until now, our inquiry has concentrated on how information moves through a technological process: from light to pixels, from pixels to facial representations, from representations to similarity scores, and from scores to alerts.

The next stage is different.

A person must interpret the result. An institution must decide what significance to attach to it. Someone may then take action.

The computer has produced an output, but the consequences of that output are shaped by a wider decision-making process.

This is the point at which the distinction between technical performance and institutional responsibility becomes unavoidable.

Imagine that the operator believes the images depict the same person. The operator informs nearby officers. Those officers approach the individual.

The person may not know that an automated system contributed to the encounter. They may see only a police officer asking questions.

From the individual's perspective, the immediate event is an exercise of institutional authority. Behind it lies a chain of technological and human activities that may not be visible.

To evaluate that encounter, we must reconstruct the chain.

Who selected the photograph against which the person's face was compared? Who configured the system? What information did the operator receive? What judgement did the operator make? What authority did the responding officer possess?

These are not variations of a single question.

They concern different decisions, different responsibilities, and different forms of evidence.

What Happened After an Alert in Bridges?

The Court of Appeal described the operational procedure used by South Wales Police.

Where AFR Locate identified a possible correspondence between a face captured by CCTV and an image on the watchlist, the two images were reviewed by a police officer acting as the system operator.

The operator assessed whether the images appeared to depict the person of interest.

If the operator rejected the possible match, no further action followed on that basis.

If the operator believed that a match had been made, officers stationed nearby could be notified and might intervene.

The judgment explained that officers could exercise statutory powers where the circumstances justified doing so.

This sequence matters because it shows that the system did not itself exercise police powers.

The software generated a possible match. A police officer reviewed it. Other officers could then take action.

The court's account therefore describes a process involving both automated analysis and human participation.

But we should not draw more from that description than it establishes.

The fact that a human reviewer was present does not, by itself, tell us how every alert was assessed, how long each review took, or whether every reviewer exercised effective independent judgement.

Those would be additional factual questions.

Likewise, the existence of human review does not establish that the facial recognition system was legally compliant.

Indeed, the Bridges appeal succeeded on grounds concerning the legal framework governing deployment, the data protection impact assessment, and the Public Sector Equality Duty.

The case demonstrates that human review may be one element of a system's safeguards without resolving every question about its lawful operation.

A Decision Is More Than a Calculation

Consider a familiar administrative situation.

A university uses software to calculate whether students satisfy an attendance requirement.

The program reads attendance records and produces a result indicating that a student has fallen below the required percentage.

That result is a calculation.

Suppose the university then prevents the student from sitting an examination.

That is an institutional action.

Between the two lies a decision about the meaning and consequences of the calculated result.

The university may have adopted a rule requiring a minimum attendance percentage. It may have established exceptions for medical circumstances. It may require a staff member to verify records before imposing a restriction.

The software does not determine the validity of those rules merely by applying them correctly.

Nor does a mathematically accurate percentage prove that the underlying attendance records are complete.

The institution must distinguish between the factual information produced by its system and the authority to act upon that information.

This distinction becomes especially important when a computer-generated result appears to carry an official recommendation.

A system may label an application as high risk, identify a transaction as suspicious, or recommend that a person receive additional scrutiny.

Such labels may influence institutional behaviour.

But the label is not itself a legal finding of wrongdoing.

It is an output generated for a particular purpose under a particular methodology.

Its appropriate use depends on the surrounding rules and circumstances.

The Institutional Decision-Making Chain

To examine an AI-assisted decision, it is useful to distinguish several stages.

First, an institution defines an objective.

A police force may seek to locate people who are wanted on warrants. A bank may seek to evaluate lending risk. A hospital may seek to identify patients requiring further clinical examination.

Second, the institution selects or develops a technological system intended to support that objective.

Third, the system processes information and produces an output.

Fourth, the output is communicated to a person or another system.

Fifth, a decision-making procedure determines how the output should be treated.

Finally, an action may follow.

These stages can be represented conceptually as:

Institutional objective → System selection and configuration → Data processing → Model output → Review or decision rule → Institutional action.

This is an explanatory framework, not a claim that every organisation follows an identical operational sequence.

Some systems make recommendations. Others automatically execute decisions. Some require multiple layers of human approval. Others operate continuously with limited human involvement.

The precise arrangement must be established from the facts.

Nevertheless, the framework helps identify a central principle:

An AI-assisted decision is not adequately understood by examining the model alone.

The institution determines the purpose for which the model is used, the authority attached to its outputs, and the procedures through which those outputs affect people.

Who Makes the Decision?

The answer may be less obvious than it first appears.

Suppose a bank uses an AI system to assess loan applications.

The system assigns an applicant a risk score.

A bank employee reviews the score and declines the application.

Who made the decision?

One answer is that the employee made the final decision because the employee selected the outcome.

But this description may be incomplete.

What if the employee was required by internal policy to reject every application above a particular risk threshold?

What if the employee could theoretically override the recommendation but lacked access to the information needed to challenge it?

What if deviations from the system's recommendation required exceptional managerial approval?

In such circumstances, the formal presence of a human decision-maker may not accurately describe the practical influence of the automated output.

The system may exert substantial control over the decision even though a human performs the final administrative act.

Conversely, an employee may genuinely evaluate the underlying evidence, consider additional circumstances, and reach a reasoned conclusion that differs from the system's recommendation.

The distinction depends on the actual process.

For legal analysis, it is therefore necessary to separate formal decision-making authority from practical decision-making influence.

Formal authority concerns who is legally or institutionally empowered to decide.

Practical influence concerns how strongly the automated output shapes the decision that is ultimately made.

These questions are related but not identical.

Human-in-the-Loop: What Does It Really Mean?

The expression human-in-the-loop is frequently used in discussions of responsible artificial intelligence.

It generally refers to arrangements in which a human participates in an operational decision process involving an automated system.

But the expression is not a guarantee of effective oversight.

Its meaning depends on the design of the process.

Consider three different arrangements.

In the first, a system produces a recommendation that a human must review before action is taken.

In the second, a system makes decisions automatically while a human monitors its operation and may intervene.

In the third, a system operates autonomously within predefined limits, while humans retain responsibility for broader governance, evaluation, and the ability to modify or discontinue its use.

These arrangements are often described using terms such as human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop, and human-in-command.

The terminology is useful for explaining different forms of human involvement, but its use is not entirely uniform across technical literature, organisational practice, and legal instruments.

Consequently, a governance document should not rely on the label alone.

It should explain the actual responsibilities and powers of the human participants.

Does the reviewer approve every result?

Can the reviewer reject a recommendation?

Can the reviewer suspend the system?

Can the reviewer obtain additional information?

Is the reviewer required to explain departures from automated advice?

These details determine whether human participation provides meaningful control.

The Difference Between Reviewing and Approving

Imagine a staff member who receives a list of applications ranked by an AI system.

The employee is instructed to review the list before approving the system's recommendations.

At first glance, the procedure appears to include human oversight.

But what does review require?

The employee might examine the underlying documents, compare the system's recommendation with the applicable criteria, and consider relevant exceptions.

Alternatively, the employee might simply click an approval button after reading the recommended outcome.

Both activities involve a human.

They do not involve the same degree of independent assessment.

Meaningful review requires more than the physical presence of a person in the workflow.

It depends on the person's capacity to understand the task, access relevant information, recognise possible errors, and exercise genuine authority.

It also depends on institutional conditions.

A reviewer handling hundreds of cases under severe time pressure may have fewer opportunities for careful examination than one with an appropriate workload and sufficient resources.

An interface that displays a large numerical score but conceals relevant uncertainty may encourage a different response from an interface that clearly communicates limitations and supporting information.

Human oversight is therefore partly an engineering and organisational design problem.

It cannot be evaluated solely by asking whether a human signature appears on the final decision.

Why People Sometimes Trust Automated Advice Too Much

A person may give particular weight to a computer-generated recommendation because it appears systematic, impartial, or mathematically sophisticated.

Sometimes such confidence is justified.

Automated systems can process large quantities of information consistently and may outperform unaided human judgement on well-defined tasks.

But confidence becomes problematic when users accept automated outputs without adequate scrutiny.

Researchers describe one relevant phenomenon as automation bias.

Automation bias occurs when reliance on automated advice contributes to errors in human judgement.

A reviewer may accept an incorrect recommendation despite having information that could support a different conclusion.

Alternatively, a reviewer may fail to notice a problem because the system did not generate an alert.

These are sometimes distinguished as errors of commission and omission.

An error of commission involves taking an inappropriate action, such as following an incorrect automated recommendation.

An error of omission involves failing to take an appropriate action, potentially because the automated system did not identify the relevant issue.

Parasuraman and Manzey's review of research on human interaction with automation examined these phenomena and emphasised the role of attention, workload, and the characteristics of automated systems.

The research does not establish that every human reviewer is biased towards automated advice.

Indeed, empirical findings differ across tasks and settings. Some studies have found that participants resist or selectively disregard algorithmic recommendations.

The correct conclusion is therefore more careful: automated advice can influence human judgement in ways that require empirical assessment, and the direction and strength of that influence depend on context.

We should not assume that a human reviewer always follows the machine.

Nor should we assume that human review automatically neutralises the machine's errors.

Both propositions require evidence.

A Real Research Problem: Human Advice Versus Algorithmic Advice

The complexity of human reliance on automated systems is illustrated by research on public-sector decision-making.

In Human–AI Interactions in Public Sector Decision Making: “Automation Bias” and “Selective Adherence” to Algorithmic Advice, researchers examined how decision-makers responded to algorithmic and human-expert recommendations in experimental settings.

The study is important partly because its findings challenge an overly simple narrative.

In one experiment involving a hypothetical personnel decision, the researchers did not find support for the expected automation-bias effect. Participants frequently overrode the advice, and differences between algorithmic and human-expert advice conditions were small and statistically insignificant in that experiment.

This does not prove that automation bias is absent from public administration.

It demonstrates that the effects of automated advice cannot be treated as universal.

The influence of a recommendation may depend on the decision task, the available information, the characteristics of the adviser, and the decision-maker's own judgement.

For our purposes, the methodological lesson is clear.

A claim that human reviewers merely rubber-stamp automated recommendations should be supported by evidence about their actual behaviour.

A claim that human oversight is effective should be supported in the same way.

Neither claim becomes true simply because it is intuitively plausible.

The Interface Can Shape the Decision

An AI system does not communicate directly with a human mind.

It presents information through an interface.

An interface is the means through which a person interacts with a technological system. It may consist of screens, buttons, text, visual indicators, alerts, or other controls.

The design of an interface can influence how information is understood.

Consider two hypothetical facial recognition interfaces.

The first displays a large message stating that a person has been identified, together with a numerical score.

The second displays a possible-match notification, the two relevant images, information about the meaning of the score, and a clear instruction that independent review is required.

The interfaces present the same general task, but they may communicate different degrees of certainty.

The first may appear to announce a conclusion.

The second makes the provisional nature of the result more explicit.

Whether either design improves decision-making would require testing. The example illustrates a design question rather than an established finding about a particular product.

A legal–technical assessment should therefore consider not only the data and model, but also the information presented to the person expected to use the output.

What did the reviewer actually see?

Was the result described as a prediction, a possible match, or a confirmed finding?

Were limitations visible?

Could the reviewer inspect the underlying evidence?

Was there a mechanism for rejecting or correcting the result?

These details may affect the practical significance of human oversight.

Authority, Discretion, and Institutional Rules

The technology may influence a decision, but the authority to act ordinarily arises from law and institutional arrangements rather than from the model itself.

In policing, a computer-generated alert does not create a new statutory power.

Officers must act within the powers available to them under the applicable legal framework.

In administrative decision-making, an automated recommendation does not independently establish that an agency has authority to impose a sanction.

In lending, a model's risk classification does not determine the full set of legal obligations governing the bank's treatment of applicants.

The distinction between technical output and legal authority is essential.

A system may identify a person as potentially relevant to an investigation. Whether officers may stop, search, arrest, or otherwise intervene requires a separate legal assessment.

The applicable rules may differ according to the nature of the intervention and the jurisdiction.

It would therefore be misleading to treat every facial recognition alert as legally equivalent to reasonable grounds for arrest.

Nor would it be correct to conclude that no alert can ever contribute relevant information to an officer's assessment.

The appropriate legal conclusion depends on the applicable power, the available evidence, and the circumstances.

The technological output is one potential source of information within that analysis.

Responsibility Begins Before the Alert

When an AI-assisted decision causes concern, attention often focuses on the person who acted upon the system's output.

But the final operator may not have controlled many of the choices that shaped the result.

Consider the stages preceding a facial recognition alert.

Someone selected the operational objective.

Someone authorised the deployment.

Someone determined which photographs could be placed on the watchlist.

Someone selected the system.

Someone established the operating procedures.

Someone determined how alerts would be reviewed.

The recognition software then performed its computational task within those arrangements.

These choices may involve different people and organisations.

Some may be made by the technology supplier. Others may be made by the institution deploying the system.

Responsibility cannot be assigned accurately without identifying the relevant duties and the actors who controlled the conduct in question.

A software developer may control aspects of model design.

A supplier may control documentation, updates, and certain technical settings.

A deploying institution may control the purpose of use, the reference database, the operating environment, and the consequences attached to outputs.

A human operator may control the immediate review of an alert.

The allocation varies across systems and contracts.

This is why a useful investigation asks not only who pressed the final button, but who made the decisions that created the conditions for that button to matter.

A Supplier's Assurance Is Not the Same as Operational Evidence

Suppose a supplier states that its AI system includes human oversight.

What does that statement establish?

It may indicate that the product provides functionality intended to support human review.

It does not necessarily prove that the deploying organisation uses the functionality effectively.

A supplier may provide an override button, but an institution may discourage employees from using it.

A system may generate detailed explanations, but operators may never consult them.

A product may support logging, but logging may be disabled or records may not be retained for a sufficient period.

The distinction is between a system's available capabilities and its actual operation.

Both may matter to governance and legal analysis.

Technical documentation can help establish what the product is designed to do.

Operational records can help establish how it was configured and used.

Policies can identify what employees were instructed to do.

Training records may show what guidance they received.

Review records may provide evidence of what they actually did.

No single document necessarily establishes the complete picture.

The task is to connect claims about governance with evidence of implementation.

The Importance of Decision Logs

How can an institution demonstrate what happened during an AI-assisted decision?

One potential source is a log.

A log is a record of events or activities within a computer system or operational process.

Depending on its design, a system may record when an input was received, which software version processed it, what output was generated, and what subsequent actions were taken.

An audit trail is a collection or sequence of records that allows relevant activities and changes to be traced.

In an AI-assisted decision process, an audit trail may help reconstruct the pathway from input to outcome.

For example, a useful record might identify the time of an alert, the relevant system configuration, the result presented to the operator, and whether the operator accepted or rejected it.

It may also record subsequent escalation or intervention.

But logs are not automatically complete or reliable.

A system may record some events and omit others.

A log may show that an employee clicked an approval button without establishing what the employee considered before doing so.

Records may be overwritten, retained for limited periods, or subject to access restrictions.

The absence of a recorded event does not always prove that the event did not occur.

The evidential value of a log depends on its design, completeness, integrity, and relationship to the proposition being examined.

These questions will become especially important when we discuss technical evidence and auditability later in the book.

Traceability: Following the Decision Backwards

Traceability is the ability to follow relationships among relevant elements of a process, such as requirements, data, system operations, decisions, and records.

In an AI-assisted decision, traceability helps answer a practical question:

Can we reconstruct how the outcome was reached?

Suppose an applicant challenges an automated recommendation used by a public authority.

A traceable process may allow investigators to identify the data used, the model version, the relevant output, the review procedure, and the final decision-maker.

Without such information, it may be difficult to determine whether the problem arose from inaccurate data, an unsuitable model, a configuration error, or an improper institutional decision.

Traceability does not require every system to retain every piece of information indefinitely.

Record retention must itself comply with applicable legal requirements, including privacy and data protection obligations.

The objective is to establish an appropriate evidential record for the system's purpose and risks.

This introduces a governance tension.

Institutions may need sufficient records to demonstrate accountability and investigate errors.

At the same time, retaining unnecessary personal information may create additional privacy risks.

Good governance must consider both requirements rather than assuming that more data retention is always better.

Human Oversight Must Be Designed, Not Merely Announced

If an institution wishes human review to operate as a safeguard, it should define what the reviewer is expected to do.

The reviewer may need sufficient training to understand the meaning of the system's output.

The reviewer may need access to information beyond the recommendation itself.

The reviewer may need authority to reject the output without inappropriate institutional pressure.

The process may need a mechanism for escalation when the evidence is uncertain.

The institution may also need to monitor whether review procedures operate as intended.

These are design and governance considerations. Their precise legal status depends on the applicable law, sector, and circumstances.

Not every system requires identical oversight arrangements.

The appropriate safeguards for a photograph-organising application will differ from those for a system contributing to decisions about policing, healthcare, employment, or access to public benefits.

The greater the potential consequences of error, the more important it becomes to examine whether the oversight arrangement is proportionate to the relevant risks and legal duties.

The question is not whether a human appears somewhere in the process.

It is whether the human's role can meaningfully influence the outcome in the way the institution claims.

When Responsibility Is Shared, It Does Not Disappear

Complex systems often involve multiple actors.

This can make responsibility difficult to analyse.

A developer may claim that the deploying institution selected the operational conditions.

The institution may claim that it relied on the supplier's assurances.

An operator may claim that the system presented the result as reliable.

Each statement may identify a relevant fact.

None automatically resolves the allocation of legal responsibility.

Different legal regimes impose different duties on different actors. Contractual obligations, statutory responsibilities, professional standards, and general principles of liability may all be relevant.

The existence of several participants does not mean that responsibility is necessarily divided equally.

Nor does it mean that every participant is legally responsible for every outcome.

The task is to identify the applicable duties, the conduct or omission attributed to each actor, the relevant evidence, and any required relationship between that conduct and the alleged harm.

Those issues will receive detailed treatment in the later chapters on liability, causation, and governance.

For now, the essential principle is that a technological system does not eliminate the need to identify human and institutional responsibilities.

It may make that inquiry more complicated, but not less necessary.

What Bridges Teaches About Institutional Choices

The significance of Bridges becomes clearer when we look beyond the facial comparison algorithm.

The Court of Appeal was concerned with the legal framework governing South Wales Police's use of the technology.

Two questions were especially important to its assessment of the discretion available to the police.

Who could be included on a watchlist?

Where could the technology be deployed?

These were institutional choices.

They were not questions that a facial recognition model could answer by calculating similarity.

A highly accurate algorithm could still operate within a framework that left excessive discretion over the selection of people or deployment locations.

The Court of Appeal concluded that the arrangements did not provide sufficiently clear legal constraints on those matters.

The resulting deficiency concerned the lawfulness of the interference with Article 8 rights.

The court also found deficiencies in the data protection impact assessment and in the discharge of the Public Sector Equality Duty.

These conclusions illustrate an important relationship between engineering and law.

Technical performance is relevant, but it is not the only measure of a system's legitimacy.

An institution must also be able to justify the purposes, procedures, and safeguards governing its use of the technology.

The legal problem may arise from a decision made before the algorithm processes a single image.

The Hidden Decision Before the Visible Decision

Imagine a facial recognition system that never makes an incorrect comparison.

This is a hypothetical assumption, not a claim about any real system.

Would its deployment necessarily be lawful?

No such conclusion follows.

Someone must still decide which people are placed on the watchlist.

Someone must decide where and when the system is used.

Someone must determine what information is collected and how it is processed.

Someone must establish the procedures for reviewing and acting upon results.

These decisions may affect individuals even if the comparison algorithm performs perfectly.

The point is not that accuracy is unimportant.

Accuracy is essential to evaluating many uses of AI.

But technical accuracy cannot substitute for lawful authority, appropriate purpose, adequate safeguards, or compliance with relevant duties.

A system can be computationally reliable and institutionally misused.

Equally, an institution may adopt carefully designed procedures while relying on a technically unsuitable model.

Effective governance requires attention to both dimensions.

A Practical Method for Reconstructing an AI-Assisted Decision

A lawyer investigating an AI-assisted decision can begin with a sequence of questions.

First, identify the institutional objective.

What was the organisation trying to achieve?

Second, identify the technological function.

What task was the system designed to perform?

Third, identify the information used.

What data entered the process, and where did those data originate?

Fourth, identify the output.

Did the system produce a score, classification, recommendation, alert, or decision?

Fifth, identify the human and organisational response.

Who received the output, what information was available to them, and what authority did they possess?

Sixth, identify the action affecting the individual.

What actually happened as a result of the process?

Finally, identify the evidence available to verify each stage.

These questions provide a structured starting point.

They do not predetermine the legal outcome.

Instead, they help separate the facts that must be established from the legal standards that will later be applied.

The method also helps avoid a common analytical mistake: attributing an institutional outcome entirely to the algorithm without examining the intervening decisions.

A system's output may have been decisive.

It may have been one factor among several.

It may have been rejected.

The evidence must establish which occurred.

From the Screen to the Street

We began this section with a police officer looking at a possible facial recognition match.

The system had produced a numerical result.

But the result was not the end of the process.

A human operator had to interpret it. Other officers might then act. Those actions occurred within an institutional framework shaped by policies, legal powers, technical arrangements, and organisational responsibilities.

The person affected by the encounter might see none of this underlying structure.

For that person, the experience could begin with a simple question from an officer.

For the lawyer, however, the inquiry must reach backwards through the process.

Why was the person examined?

Why was that reference image in the database?

What did the system actually report?

What did the operator understand?

What did the officer know before acting?

Which rules governed the intervention?

And what records can establish the answers?

These questions reveal why the relationship between artificial intelligence and law cannot be reduced to a contest between human judgement and machine accuracy.

The relevant process is sociotechnical: it combines technological components with human decisions, institutional structures, and social consequences.

A system may influence a decision without formally making it.

A human may formally make a decision without exercising meaningful independence.

A technically correct output may be used unlawfully.

And an incorrect output may be detected before it causes harm.

To distinguish these possibilities, legal professionals must understand both the technology and the institution in which it operates.

The next step is to examine the legal framework itself.

We have identified the points at which an AI system may influence institutional action. We must now ask when those activities engage legally protected rights, powers, and duties.

At what point does the use of an AI system become a question of law?

CHAPTER ONE

1.6 Where the Legal Questions Begin

The Person Who Was Never on the Watchlist

Imagine a person walking along Queen Street in Cardiff.

They are not wanted by the police. They have not been identified as a suspect. Their photograph is not on the watchlist. They are simply passing through a public place.

A camera captures their face.

Software detects the face, extracts numerical information from the image, and compares that information with representations of people included on the police watchlist.

No match is found.

The system deletes the facial information generated for the comparison.

The person continues walking.

Nothing else happens.

Has anything legally significant occurred?

The question may seem surprising. There was no arrest, no accusation, and no apparent mistake. The system did not even identify the person as someone the police were seeking.

Yet the answer cannot be determined simply by observing that no intervention followed.

The person's image was captured and processed. Information derived from their facial characteristics was used in an identification procedure. The processing occurred as part of an operation conducted by a public authority.

Those facts may be legally significant even though the system produced no alert.

This is one of the most important lessons of R (Bridges) v Chief Constable of South Wales Police.

The legal inquiry did not begin only when facial recognition software identified a possible match. It began with the police's use of technology to collect and process information about members of the public.

To understand why, we must distinguish a technical event from a legally relevant event.

A technical event is something that happens within the operation of a system: an image is captured, a database is searched, a score is calculated, or a record is deleted.

A legally relevant event is a fact or activity that may engage a right, duty, power, prohibition, or other legal rule.

The same occurrence can be both.

The capture of a facial image is a technical operation. Depending on the circumstances and applicable law, it may also constitute processing of personal data or an interference with the right to respect for private life.

The law does not wait for the computer to announce that it has made a mistake.

Sometimes the activity that makes the system useful is itself the activity that requires legal justification.

Why Being in Public Does Not End the Privacy Question

A common argument begins with a simple observation.

People can see one another in public.

If a person walks through a shopping street, others may observe their appearance. Police officers may also observe people in public places as part of their ordinary duties.

Why, then, should a camera using facial recognition raise a privacy question?

The answer requires us to distinguish visibility from unrestricted technological processing.

A person's face may be visible without every possible use of information derived from that face being legally equivalent.

Consider three situations.

In the first, an officer observes a person walking past.

In the second, a camera records footage of the street.

In the third, a camera supplies images to software that extracts facial biometric information and compares it automatically with a police watchlist.

All three situations concern people in a public place.

But they do not involve identical activities.

The third adds automated biometric processing and comparison. It can operate rapidly and at scale, including in relation to people who are not suspected of wrongdoing.

The Court of Appeal recognised that this technological capability could not simply be equated with ordinary police observation or conventional photography.

The court examined the nature of the processing, its scale, and the legal safeguards governing its use.

This does not mean that every observation of a person in public infringes privacy rights.

Nor does it mean that all forms of public surveillance are prohibited.

The point is more precise: the fact that an individual is visible in a public place does not, by itself, settle the legal questions raised by systematic biometric processing.

The legal analysis must consider what the technology does with the information.

The Right to Respect for Private Life

Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights protects the right to respect for private and family life, home, and correspondence.

The right is incorporated into the domestic legal framework relevant to Bridges through the Human Rights Act 1998.

Article 8 is not an absolute prohibition on interference by public authorities.

The Convention permits certain interferences where the requirements of Article 8(2) are satisfied.

Those requirements include a legal basis for the interference and its justification by reference to specified legitimate aims and the applicable standard of necessity in a democratic society.

For a reader unfamiliar with human rights law, an important distinction must be made between the existence of a right and the circumstances in which interference with that right may be justified.

A right may be engaged without the interference necessarily being unlawful.

The first question is whether the activity falls within the protection of the right.

The next question is whether the interference satisfies the conditions imposed by law.

These are separate stages of legal reasoning.

In Bridges, the courts accepted that the use of AFR Locate interfered with Article 8 rights.

The Court of Appeal's central disagreement with the Divisional Court concerned whether that interference was sufficiently regulated to meet the requirement that it be in accordance with the law.

This distinction is essential to understanding the judgment.

The Court of Appeal did not conclude that every use of facial recognition necessarily violates Article 8.

It found that the legal framework governing the particular use of AFR Locate failed to impose sufficiently clear limits on important aspects of police discretion.

What Counts as an Interference?

The word interference may sound as though it requires an officer to stop someone, enter their home, or seize their property.

In human rights law, its meaning can be broader.

An interference may arise from state activity affecting a protected interest, even without physical intervention.

In Bridges, the initial capture and processing of facial biometric information was important.

The Divisional Court had accepted that Article 8 was engaged by the gathering and processing of biometric information, even where the information was retained only momentarily.

The Court of Appeal proceeded on that basis.

This matters because the technology processed information about people who did not appear on the watchlist and whose faces generated no alerts.

The absence of a match did not mean that no processing had occurred.

Nor did immediate deletion erase the fact that the processing had taken place.

Consider a simple analogy.

Suppose an organisation opens a confidential document, reads its contents, and immediately destroys its copy.

The destruction may limit future access to the document. It does not change the fact that the information was accessed.

Similarly, deleting biometric information after comparison may reduce retention-related risks without eliminating the legal significance of its initial collection and use.

The analogy does not determine the legal outcome. It illustrates the difference between processing information and retaining it.

The two activities may engage different legal questions.

The Difference Between a Legal Power and a Lawful Exercise of That Power

An institution may possess a general legal power to perform an activity.

That does not necessarily mean that every exercise of the power is lawful.

This distinction is familiar in public law.

A police officer may possess powers to investigate crime. But the existence of investigative powers does not remove the need to comply with the conditions governing their exercise.

A public authority may be authorised to collect information for legitimate purposes. But its collection practices may still be subject to legal limits.

The same principle applies to technology.

A police force may have a legitimate interest in identifying people wanted on warrants.

That objective does not automatically justify every method of identification or every configuration of a surveillance system.

The legal inquiry must consider both the source of authority and the safeguards governing its use.

In Bridges, the Court of Appeal did not hold that the police lacked all legal power to use facial recognition.

The court accepted the existence of a legal framework that included common-law police powers, legislation, codes of practice, and local policies.

The problem concerned whether that framework adequately constrained the exercise of discretion in the circumstances.

This is an important difference.

A legal system may recognise a public authority's general capacity to act while requiring greater clarity about how a particular intrusive capability is exercised.

What Does “In Accordance with the Law” Mean?

The expression in accordance with the law is central to Article 8 analysis.

It involves more than asking whether an official can point to some legal rule permitting the activity.

The applicable legal framework must possess sufficient quality to govern the interference.

Among other matters, the law must be accessible and sufficiently foreseeable in its application, and it must provide adequate safeguards against arbitrary interference.

Foreseeability does not mean that every person must be able to predict every individual decision made by a public authority.

Rather, the legal framework must define the relevant powers and constraints with sufficient clarity for the circumstances.

The required degree of precision depends on the nature of the activity and the risks it creates.

Some official decisions necessarily involve discretion.

The law does not require every possible factual situation to be exhaustively listed in legislation.

But discretion must be governed.

A system that permits officials to make important choices without sufficiently clear criteria may create a risk of arbitrary interference.

That was central to the Court of Appeal's reasoning in Bridges.

The court examined whether the arrangements provided adequate constraints on the police's choices concerning watchlists and deployment locations.

These were not peripheral administrative details.

They determined which members of the public could be subjected to automated biometric comparison and in which places the technology would operate.

Who Can Be Placed on the Watchlist?

A facial recognition system compares images against a reference collection.

In Bridges, that collection was a police watchlist.

We have already examined the watchlist as a technical component of the identification process.

Now we must examine it as an exercise of institutional discretion.

The watchlist determines whose identities the system is configured to seek.

If the police include a particular person's photograph, the system may attempt to identify that person when they pass within range of the cameras.

The decision to include the photograph therefore has consequences before any live comparison occurs.

It defines the targets of the identification process.

The Court of Appeal considered the breadth of the categories under which individuals could be included on watchlists.

Some categories concerned people wanted on warrants or suspected of criminal offences.

Others were broader, including people considered of possible interest for intelligence purposes.

The question was not whether the police could ever lawfully seek such individuals.

It was whether the legal and policy framework imposed sufficiently clear criteria on the exercise of that choice.

The court concluded that the arrangements left too much discretion concerning who could be placed on a watchlist.

The lesson extends beyond facial recognition.

Whenever an AI system is used to select people for attention, investigation, or intervention, the selection criteria may be as legally important as the model that processes the data.

A technically accurate system may still operate on a reference population selected under inadequate safeguards.

The model's performance cannot cure every defect in the institutional decision that defines its targets.

Where Can the Technology Be Used?

The second important question concerned deployment locations.

Facial recognition cameras can be positioned in different environments.

They may operate at a public event, a transport hub, a shopping street, or another location.

The choice affects who is exposed to the system and the circumstances in which their information is processed.

A deployment at a particular event may have a different factual justification from continuous operation across a large urban area.

The number of people affected, the purpose of the operation, and the surrounding circumstances may differ.

The Court of Appeal examined whether South Wales Police's policies sufficiently constrained the decision about where AFR Locate could be deployed.

It concluded that they did not.

The issue was not that every deployment location must be specified individually in primary legislation.

The concern was that the framework did not provide sufficiently clear limits on a significant area of operational discretion.

This distinction is important.

Legal oversight need not require the legislature to make every operational decision.

But the law may require the authority's discretion to be structured through adequate rules and safeguards.

The greater the capacity of a technology to affect large numbers of people, the more important it may become to examine how decisions about its deployment are controlled.

Necessity and Proportionality: Asking More Than Whether a System Is Useful

Public authorities often adopt technologies because they may improve efficiency, accuracy, or operational capability.

But usefulness is not the same as legal necessity.

A technology may assist an institution in achieving a legitimate objective without every proposed use of that technology being legally justified.

In human rights analysis, necessity and proportionality are important concepts.

Necessity requires more than showing that an activity is convenient or technologically possible.

Proportionality concerns the relationship between the objective pursued, the means adopted, and the impact on protected rights.

A structured proportionality inquiry may examine the importance of the objective, the rational connection between the measure and that objective, the availability of less intrusive alternatives, and the balance between the measure's benefits and its impact on rights.

The precise formulation and application depend on the relevant legal framework and authorities.

Consider a hypothetical example.

A public authority wants to identify a small number of people who may present a serious security concern at a particular event.

Facial recognition might assist that objective.

But legal analysis would still need to consider the scope of the deployment, the people affected, the available alternatives, the safeguards, and the nature of the risks.

It would be insufficient simply to say that the technology can identify faces quickly.

The ability to perform an operation is not the same as justification for performing it.

A critical qualification is necessary in relation to Bridges.

The Court of Appeal allowed the first ground of appeal because the interference was not in accordance with the law. It rejected the separate proportionality ground.

We must therefore not present the judgment as though the Court of Appeal had held that the use of AFR Locate was disproportionate.

The court's findings on the quality of the legal framework and its conclusions on proportionality were distinct.

Data Protection: The Law of Processing, Not Merely Storage

The privacy inquiry is closely connected to data protection law, but the two should not be treated as identical.

Data protection law regulates the processing of personal data.

Personal data generally concerns information relating to an identified or identifiable individual, subject to the definition in the applicable legislation.

Processing is a broad concept that can encompass activities such as collecting, recording, organising, consulting, using, disclosing, and erasing information.

The precise statutory definition must be taken from the relevant legal regime.

A system may therefore process personal data even if it does not permanently store the information.

This is particularly important for technologies that operate in real time.

A facial recognition system may capture an image, extract a biometric representation, compare it against stored references, and delete certain information within moments.

The speed of the operation does not make it legally invisible.

In Bridges, the relevant data protection analysis concerned the legislation applicable to the police's law-enforcement processing, including Part 3 of the Data Protection Act 2018.

This distinction matters because different processing activities may fall under different data protection regimes.

It would be inaccurate to analyse the police's law-enforcement processing in this case as though every provision of the general UK GDPR regime applied to it in exactly the same way.

The applicable statutory framework must be identified before the legal requirements are stated.

Why Biometric Data Require Particular Attention

Biometric systems use information derived from physical, physiological, or behavioural characteristics for recognition or identification.

Examples include facial recognition, fingerprint recognition, and certain voice-recognition systems.

Not every photograph is automatically equivalent to biometric data processed for unique identification.

The legal classification depends on the relevant definition and the processing performed.

Under the Data Protection Act 2018, biometric data are defined by reference to personal data resulting from specific technical processing relating to an individual's characteristics that allows or confirms unique identification.

The purpose and technical processing therefore matter.

A photograph stored in a family album and a facial representation generated for automated identification are not necessarily treated identically merely because both involve a person's face.

In Bridges, the use of AFR Locate involved the processing of facial biometric information for identification purposes.

The law-enforcement data protection regime imposed specific requirements for sensitive processing.

These requirements were not satisfied merely because the police had a legitimate interest in preventing or detecting crime.

The statutory framework required attention to the conditions under which such processing could lawfully occur.

For legal professionals, the practical lesson is to identify the information involved, the operation performed on it, the purpose of that operation, and the legal category into which the processing falls.

A generic statement that a system uses photographs may conceal legally significant technical processing.

The Data Protection Impact Assessment: A Document That Must Examine Reality

A data protection impact assessment, commonly abbreviated DPIA, is a structured assessment of risks associated with personal-data processing.

Its function is not simply to record that an organisation intends to comply with the law.

It is intended to support examination of the proposed processing and the risks it may create.

A meaningful assessment begins by understanding the activity being assessed.

What information will be collected?

Whose information will be processed?

For what purpose?

What operations will be performed?

What risks may arise?

What safeguards are proposed?

These questions require factual and technical understanding.

An assessment based on an incomplete description of the system may fail to identify important risks.

This was significant in Bridges.

The Court of Appeal concluded that South Wales Police's DPIA did not comply with the relevant requirements of section 64(3)(b) and (c) of the Data Protection Act 2018.

The finding was connected to the court's conclusion that the interference with Article 8 rights was not in accordance with the law.

It would be misleading to describe the judgment as a finding that every aspect of the police's data protection arrangements was unlawful.

The court addressed particular grounds of appeal and reached particular conclusions.

Nevertheless, the case illustrates why an impact assessment must be grounded in an accurate understanding of the processing and its legal context.

A document cannot provide meaningful assurance merely by bearing the title impact assessment.

Its substance matters.

Why an Impact Assessment Is Not a Technical Performance Test

A DPIA and a technical evaluation may examine some of the same system characteristics, but they serve different purposes.

A technical performance test may measure identification errors under specified conditions.

A DPIA examines risks to individuals' rights and freedoms arising from processing personal data, together with the relevant safeguards.

A system may achieve strong benchmark results while still raising significant data protection concerns.

Conversely, a DPIA may identify and address privacy risks without proving that the model is technically accurate.

The two forms of assessment are complementary.

Suppose an institution deploys facial recognition cameras in a crowded public area.

A technical evaluation might ask how often the system generates incorrect identification candidates.

A data protection assessment might ask whether the processing is lawful, necessary for its stated purpose, appropriately limited, and subject to suitable safeguards.

The evaluation of technical performance can inform the assessment of risk.

But the legal assessment cannot be reduced to a performance score.

This distinction will later become central to our discussion of verifiable AI governance.

Different claims require different evidence.

Equality: When the Duty Is to Investigate a Risk

The third successful ground of appeal in Bridges concerned the Public Sector Equality Duty.

This duty is established by section 149 of the Equality Act 2010.

In broad terms, it requires public authorities, when exercising their functions, to have due regard to specified equality objectives, including the need to eliminate prohibited discrimination and advance equality of opportunity.

The duty is concerned with the process through which public authorities consider relevant equality matters.

It does not guarantee that every decision will produce an identical outcome for every group.

Nor does it mean that every difference in a technological system's performance automatically establishes unlawful discrimination.

The question in Bridges was more specific.

There was a recognised possibility that facial recognition systems might perform differently across demographic groups.

The relevant characteristics in the appeal were race and sex.

South Wales Police had not taken the reasonable steps required to investigate whether the particular software might operate with differential accuracy in those respects.

The Court of Appeal held that the police had failed to comply with the Public Sector Equality Duty.

This finding is significant precisely because the court did not need to establish that the software had actually produced discriminatory outcomes.

The duty required the authority to give proper consideration to the relevant risk.

Failure to conduct the necessary inquiry could itself constitute a breach of the procedural duty.

The Difference Between Proving Discrimination and Failing to Investigate It

Imagine two distinct legal claims.

In the first, a person alleges that an automated system treated them unlawfully because of a protected characteristic.

That claim may require evidence about the treatment, the relevant legal test, and the relationship between the characteristic and the outcome.

In the second, a public authority is challenged for failing to investigate a foreseeable risk of discriminatory effects before deploying a system.

That claim concerns whether the authority complied with a duty to consider relevant equality implications.

The evidence required for the two claims is not identical.

A claimant alleging unlawful discriminatory treatment may need to establish facts supporting the applicable discrimination claim.

A claimant challenging compliance with the Public Sector Equality Duty may focus on the authority's decision-making process, the information it obtained, and the inquiries it undertook.

The second claim does not automatically establish the first.

This distinction was expressly important in Bridges.

The appeal did not proceed on the basis that the particular facial recognition software had been proved to discriminate against a demographic group.

The successful equality ground concerned the adequacy of the police's investigation of the risk.

For a lawyer working with AI systems, this reveals an important category of legal–technical evidence.

Sometimes the relevant question is not whether an error has already occurred.

It is whether the institution took appropriate steps to discover whether a material risk existed.

What Does “Due Regard” Require in Practice?

The expression due regard is a legal standard, not a technical metric.

It requires appropriate consideration of the relevant statutory equality objectives.

The Court of Appeal referred to established public-law principles explaining that the duty must be performed substantively and with rigour.

It is not fulfilled merely by completing a form or reciting a general commitment to equality.

The duty is also continuing, and the relevant authority cannot simply delegate away its responsibility for compliance.

Where necessary information is unavailable, proper consideration may require steps to obtain it.

Applied to an AI system, these principles raise practical questions.

Did the authority recognise the possibility of differential performance?

Did it examine relevant evidence?

Did it seek information from the supplier?

Did it consider whether independent testing was necessary?

Did it assess the significance of any limitations in the available evidence?

Did it revisit the issue as further information became available?

The exact steps required depend on the circumstances and the applicable legal duty.

The law does not necessarily prescribe one universal testing procedure for every AI system.

But a public authority cannot assume that the absence of evidence of bias is equivalent to evidence that no relevant risk exists.

The distinction between not knowing and having investigated is crucial.

What If the Supplier Says the Model Is Accurate?

Suppose a public authority purchases facial recognition software.

The supplier provides a brochure stating that the system performs accurately.

Can the authority rely on that statement to discharge its legal duties?

The answer depends on the duty, the evidence supplied, and the circumstances.

A general performance claim may not reveal whether the system was tested across relevant demographic groups.

It may not identify the testing conditions, error measures, or populations involved.

It may not explain whether the results apply to the version and configuration being deployed.

The authority may therefore need further information.

This does not mean that every supplier claim is unreliable.

It means that the relevance and sufficiency of the claim must be assessed.

A statement about overall accuracy does not necessarily answer a question about differential accuracy.

A test conducted on one population may not answer a question about another.

A benchmark result may not establish how the complete system will perform in a particular operational environment.

The legal duty identifies the question that matters.

The technical evidence must be capable of answering that question.

This is the relationship between legal requirements and engineering evidence that the book will develop in greater depth.

The Risk of Treating Technical Uncertainty as Legal Permission

An institution may not know whether a particular system presents a significant risk.

That uncertainty does not always prohibit deployment.

But neither does it automatically justify proceeding without further inquiry.

The legal significance of uncertainty depends on the applicable obligations, the potential consequences, and the evidence reasonably available.

Consider a hypothetical public authority considering a system that recommends which applicants should receive additional investigation.

The authority knows that the model may perform differently across relevant groups, but it has not examined that possibility.

The authority might argue that there is no proof of discrimination.

That statement may be factually accurate.

But it does not answer whether the authority has fulfilled a separate legal duty to investigate foreseeable equality risks.

The distinction between substantive wrongdoing and procedural compliance is important.

A legal system may impose duties designed to improve the quality and accountability of decisions before harm is demonstrated.

Those duties are particularly relevant where complex technologies make risks difficult to observe directly.

Four Questions, Four Different Legal Inquiries

The Bridges litigation allows us to distinguish several legal questions that can arise from the same technological system.

The first concerns protected rights.

Does the collection or processing of information interfere with a right, such as respect for private life?

The second concerns legal authority.

What legal powers permit the activity, and what constraints govern their exercise?

The third concerns data protection.

What information is processed, for what purpose, and under which statutory conditions and safeguards?

The fourth concerns equality duties.

Has the authority properly considered the possibility that the system may affect people differently in legally relevant ways?

These inquiries overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

A system might have a lawful purpose yet fail to satisfy a procedural duty.

It might be technically accurate yet operate under insufficiently clear deployment rules.

It might use appropriate retention periods while still raising questions about the initial collection of biometric information.

It might include human review without resolving whether the institution adequately investigated demographic performance.

The existence of one safeguard does not automatically answer every legal question.

The analysis must identify each applicable duty and the facts relevant to its satisfaction.

What the Court Actually Decided

The outcome of Bridges must be stated precisely.

The Court of Appeal allowed the appeal on three grounds.

First, the use of AFR Locate interfered with Article 8 rights in a manner that was not in accordance with the law, because the governing framework did not adequately constrain important aspects of police discretion.

Second, the data protection impact assessment did not comply with the relevant statutory requirements identified by the court.

Third, South Wales Police had failed to comply with the Public Sector Equality Duty.

The court rejected the separate grounds concerning proportionality and the additional data protection challenge.

The remedy was declaratory relief.

A declaration is a judicial statement determining the legal position or identifying the relevant legal wrong. It does not necessarily involve an award of damages or a prohibitory order.

The court's decision should therefore not be described as a universal ban on facial recognition.

Nor should it be presented as a finding that the particular software was proved to be demographically biased.

The judgment established more specific propositions about the legal framework, the impact assessment, and the equality duty.

Its significance lies partly in demonstrating that those matters can be decisive even without proof that a particular facial comparison was wrong.

From Legal Rules to Questions About the System

We can now see how a lawyer begins to translate legal concerns into questions about technology.

If the issue concerns interference with privacy, the lawyer may need to establish what information the system collected, how it processed that information, and who was affected.

If the issue concerns the adequacy of legal safeguards, the lawyer may need to examine the criteria governing watchlist inclusion and deployment decisions.

If the issue concerns data protection compliance, the lawyer may need to understand the processing operations, their purposes, the applicable statutory regime, and the substance of the impact assessment.

If the issue concerns equality duties, the lawyer may need evidence about the risks considered, the performance information obtained, and the steps taken to investigate relevant differences.

These are not merely technical questions.

They are technical questions selected because they bear upon legal standards.

That selection is an essential professional skill.

A lawyer does not need to request every available engineering document in every dispute.

The lawyer must first identify the legal proposition requiring proof.

Only then can the appropriate evidence be selected.

The Danger of Beginning with the Wrong Question

Imagine that a lawyer investigating a facial recognition deployment begins by asking the supplier to disclose the model's source code.

Source code consists of instructions written in a programming language and may be relevant to certain technical investigations.

But is it necessarily the first evidence needed?

Not always.

If the central legal issue is whether the police had sufficiently clear rules governing watchlist inclusion, the most immediately relevant evidence may be the governing policies and operational criteria.

If the issue is whether the authority investigated demographic performance, the relevant evidence may include testing reports, supplier inquiries, risk assessments, and internal decision records.

If the issue concerns a disputed individual alert, images, logs, configuration records, and expert analysis may become important.

Source code may be relevant in some circumstances, but it is not a universal substitute for understanding the legal question.

The opposite mistake is equally problematic.

A lawyer may examine the written policies and conclude that the system is adequately governed without investigating whether those policies were implemented.

A policy requiring human review does not prove that meaningful review occurred.

A policy requiring performance monitoring does not prove that monitoring was conducted.

A statement promising lawful processing does not establish that every processing operation was lawful.

The legal inquiry must therefore connect formal requirements with evidence of actual operation.

The First Legal–Technical Principle

The relationship between artificial intelligence and law becomes clearer when we formulate a basic principle:

The legal significance of a technological system depends not only on what the system is capable of doing, but also on what an institution uses it to do, how that use affects legally protected interests, and which duties govern the activity.

This principle explains why a technically simple system may raise serious legal questions.

An ordinary spreadsheet can affect access to public benefits if an authority relies on it to determine eligibility.

A conventional database can create privacy risks if it contains sensitive personal information and is used without appropriate safeguards.

A machine-learning model may contribute to an important decision even where its output is formally described as a recommendation.

The degree of technological sophistication is therefore not the sole determinant of legal significance.

The relevant relationship is between the system, its use, the people affected, and the applicable law.

Artificial intelligence can intensify familiar legal problems by increasing the scale, speed, complexity, or apparent authority of automated processing.

But the legal inquiry must still begin with the relevant rights and duties.

Returning to the Person in the Street

The person who passed the camera in Cardiff may never have known that their face was processed.

They may never have appeared on a watchlist.

They may never have been approached by an officer.

Their biometric information may have been deleted almost immediately.

Yet the processing formed part of an institutional activity capable of engaging legal rights and duties.

That is why the absence of an adverse decision does not necessarily mean the absence of a legal question.

The law may regulate the collection of information before an output is generated.

It may regulate the discretion governing deployment before a camera is activated.

It may require investigation of risks before a system is relied upon.

And it may require adequate safeguards even where the technology appears to operate correctly.

We have now reached a turning point in the chapter.

We understand the basic technology.

We understand how an automated output can influence institutional action.

And we have identified the legal questions that can arise before, during, and after that action.

But identifying a legal question is not the same as proving its answer.

If a lawyer alleges that a system was used under inadequate safeguards, what documents would establish the governing rules?

If a public authority claims that it investigated demographic performance, what evidence would demonstrate that investigation?

If a person disputes an automated identification, which technical records could help reconstruct what happened?

And if relevant evidence is unavailable, what conclusions can reasonably be drawn from its absence?

Those are the questions that lead us from legal principles to technical evidence.

CHAPTER ONE

1.7 What Evidence Would We Need?

A Claim Is Not Yet Evidence

A police force announces that its facial recognition system is accurate, carefully controlled, and subject to human oversight.

A technology supplier explains that the software has been tested.

An internal policy states that officers must review every possible match before taking action.

A data protection impact assessment describes the safeguards intended to protect members of the public.

Each statement may be important.

But what does each actually establish?

A supplier's assurance may describe the capabilities of a product. A policy may describe the procedure an institution expects its employees to follow. An impact assessment may record risks and proposed safeguards. A performance report may measure a system under specified testing conditions.

None of these documents necessarily establishes what happened during a particular deployment.

This is the central evidential problem of AI-assisted decision-making.

The system may be complex, but the legal question is often precise.

Was a particular person's information processed?

Was the relevant watchlist compiled according to lawful criteria?

Was an alert generated?

Did a human reviewer independently assess it?

Did the institution investigate a foreseeable risk before deploying the technology?

To answer such questions, the lawyer must move beyond general descriptions of artificial intelligence.

The task is to identify the specific facts requiring proof and the evidence capable of establishing them.

This is where technical literacy becomes an instrument of legal reasoning.

What Do We Mean by Evidence?

In ordinary language, evidence is information that supports or challenges a proposition.

A proposition is a statement that may be true or false.

For example:

“The facial recognition system generated an alert concerning this person.”

That is a factual proposition.

“The alert was incorrect.”

That is another.

“The police acted unlawfully.”

That is a legal conclusion, which may depend on several factual and legal propositions.

These statements should not be treated as interchangeable.

The first concerns whether an event occurred.

The second concerns the accuracy of a technical result.

The third concerns the application of law to established facts.

Different evidence may be needed for each.

In legal proceedings, the relevance, admissibility, weight, and sufficiency of evidence are governed by the applicable procedural and substantive law.

Those concepts also require distinction.

Relevance concerns whether information has a logical or legally recognised connection to a matter in issue.

Admissibility concerns whether the applicable rules permit the evidence to be received or considered.

Weight concerns the significance or persuasive value assigned to evidence.

Sufficiency concerns whether the evidence meets the applicable standard for establishing a proposition or supporting a legal outcome.

A document may be relevant without being admissible.

Admissible evidence may be given little weight.

And a collection of credible evidence may still be insufficient to prove the proposition for which it is offered.

These distinctions will receive detailed treatment in Chapter Eight.

For now, we need a more basic principle:

The existence of technical information does not automatically make it proof of the legal proposition being asserted.

Begin with the Proposition, Not the Document

Imagine that a lawyer is instructed to challenge an automated identification.

An instinctive response might be to demand the algorithm's source code.

But source code is not necessarily the most useful starting point.

Before requesting evidence, the lawyer should identify the precise issue.

Is the client disputing that their face was processed?

Are they disputing the identity suggested by the system?

Are they challenging the legal authority for the deployment?

Are they alleging that the police failed to investigate discriminatory risks?

Each question leads to a different evidential inquiry.

If the issue concerns whether an alert occurred, operational records may be particularly relevant.

If the issue concerns whether two images depict the same person, the images, comparison result, and expert examination may matter.

If the issue concerns the legality of watchlist inclusion, the criteria and records governing that inclusion may be central.

If the issue concerns the Public Sector Equality Duty, the authority's inquiries, assessments, and information available at the relevant time may be more important than the source code.

This does not mean that source code is never relevant.

It means that evidence should be selected because it can help answer a defined question.

A technically impressive document may have little value if it does not address the proposition in dispute.

Reconstructing the System Before Reconstructing the Event

A lawyer cannot evaluate a disputed output without understanding the system that produced it.

The first task is therefore to establish the relevant system architecture.

Architecture, in this context, means the arrangement of a system's components and the relationships among them.

For a facial recognition deployment, the architecture may include cameras, image-processing software, a facial recognition model, a reference database, comparison procedures, alert mechanisms, operator interfaces, and record-retention processes.

The lawyer does not need to design these components.

But they must understand their functions well enough to identify where relevant information may exist.

A system architecture diagram may help.

Such a diagram represents the principal components and the movement of information between them.

For example, it may show that camera images are sent to a processing component, which produces facial representations, compares them with stored references, and sends qualifying results to an operator interface.

The diagram can help identify possible sources of evidence.

However, it does not necessarily establish that the system operated exactly as depicted during the disputed event.

A design diagram describes an arrangement.

Evidence of actual operation requires further investigation.

This is the first major distinction between design evidence and operational evidence.

Design Evidence: What the System Was Supposed to Do

Design evidence describes the intended structure, functions, and behaviour of a system.

It may include technical specifications, architecture diagrams, operating manuals, model documentation, configuration guides, and system requirements.

A technical specification is a document describing defined characteristics or requirements of a product or system.

A requirement states a condition or capability that the system is expected to satisfy.

For example, a requirement might state that facial templates generated from non-matching live images must be deleted immediately after comparison.

A specification might explain the software mechanism intended to implement that requirement.

A test report might describe an examination of whether the mechanism worked under defined conditions.

These documents are related, but they do not prove the same thing.

The requirement establishes what was expected.

The specification describes how the system was intended to meet that expectation.

The test report may provide evidence about whether the implementation satisfied it under the test conditions.

Operational records may then help determine whether the deployed system behaved accordingly.

A lawyer should not treat these different forms of evidence as interchangeable.

The distinction matters whenever an organisation claims that a safeguard existed.

The safeguard may have been written into a policy without being implemented.

It may have been implemented without being enabled.

It may have been enabled but malfunctioned.

Or it may have operated correctly.

Each possibility requires different evidence.

Operational Evidence: What Actually Happened

Operational evidence concerns the system's behaviour during use.

It may include logs, transaction records, generated outputs, operator actions, deployment records, incident reports, and other contemporaneous information.

A log is a record of events within a technological system.

Depending on its design, a log may record when an event occurred, what operation was performed, and what result followed.

Consider a hypothetical facial recognition alert.

A relevant record might show that an image was processed at 14:32, that the system returned a particular watchlist candidate, and that the operator accepted or rejected the possible match.

Such a record could help reconstruct the sequence of events.

But the evidential significance of a log depends on what the system actually records.

A log may identify that an alert was displayed without preserving the underlying image.

It may record an operator's selection without revealing the reasoning behind that selection.

It may record a software version without preserving every configuration parameter.

The lawyer must therefore ask not simply whether logs exist, but what each recorded field means.

A field is an individual item of information within a structured record.

For example, a log might contain fields for event time, event type, system identifier, and outcome.

The meaning of those fields should be established through documentation or other reliable evidence.

Otherwise, a technical record may be misunderstood.

The Problem of Missing Logs

Suppose a person alleges that a facial recognition system processed their face.

The institution searches its records but finds no corresponding alert.

Can it conclude that the person's face was never processed?

Not necessarily.

A system may process many faces without generating alerts.

If the system records only alerts, the absence of an alert record cannot establish that no facial comparison occurred.

Similarly, a system may delete certain biometric information immediately after processing.

The absence of a retained biometric template may therefore be consistent with the system's intended operation.

This is particularly relevant to Bridges.

South Wales Police could not establish whether Edward Bridges's face had actually been captured and processed during the relevant deployments.

The litigation nevertheless proceeded on the basis described in the judgment, without treating that uncertainty as proof that his face had been processed.

The case therefore illustrates an important evidential limit.

A court may address the lawfulness of a system's operation without possessing a complete record of every individual processing event.

But the absence of an individual record must not be transformed into a positive factual assertion.

Not being able to establish that an event occurred is not the same as establishing that it did not occur.

The distinction is fundamental to responsible legal reasoning.

Evidence of Deletion Is Not the Same as Absence of Processing

Imagine that a system is configured to delete facial templates immediately after comparison.

The institution produces a policy stating that deletion occurs automatically.

What does the policy establish?

It may establish the intended retention rule.

Does it prove that the rule operated correctly during every deployment?

Not by itself.

The institution might also produce technical documentation explaining the deletion mechanism.

That may help establish how the software was designed.

A test report may demonstrate that the mechanism performed as expected under specified conditions.

Operational monitoring may provide additional evidence about its functioning during deployment.

These forms of evidence strengthen different parts of the account.

Even then, an investigator should distinguish between proving that a deletion mechanism operated and proving that a particular person's information was never processed.

A system may collect, analyse, and delete information in a very short period.

The deletion may be a meaningful privacy safeguard.

It does not eliminate the initial processing event.

This distinction connects the technical evidence directly to the legal analysis developed in Section 1.6.

The lawyer must know which activity the relevant law regulates.

Evidence About the Watchlist

In a one-to-many facial identification system, the watchlist is central to the operation.

It is also a potential source of legal controversy.

The lawyer may need to examine the criteria governing inclusion, the authority responsible for selecting entries, and the records showing why particular individuals were included.

A watchlist policy may identify permissible categories.

An inclusion record may show the stated reason for adding a particular person.

An approval record may identify who authorised the inclusion.

A versioned watchlist may help establish which entries were active during a particular deployment.

These records answer different questions.

The policy concerns the rules.

The inclusion record concerns their application to a person.

The approval record concerns responsibility.

The historical version concerns what reference data were available to the system at a particular time.

This distinction matters because a current watchlist does not necessarily establish what was on the list during an earlier deployment.

Data may have been added, removed, or corrected.

A reliable reconstruction may therefore require historical records rather than the present state of the database.

In Bridges, the Court of Appeal's concern about watchlists focused on the breadth of discretion left to police officers concerning who could be included.

The relevant legal question was not answered by the recognition algorithm's mathematical performance.

It required examination of the governing rules and the categories they permitted.

Evidence About Deployment Decisions

The location and timing of a deployment may also require proof.

A deployment record might identify where the cameras operated, the dates and times of use, the stated objective, and the officer responsible for authorisation.

An operational plan might explain why the location was selected.

A risk assessment might identify relevant concerns and proposed safeguards.

An approval record might show whether the required decision-making procedure was followed.

These documents could be important where the legality of the deployment depends on the scope of institutional discretion and the reasons for its exercise.

But their evidential value depends on their content.

A record stating that an operation was authorised does not necessarily establish that the authorisation was legally sufficient.

A document describing a legitimate objective does not necessarily establish that the deployment complied with every applicable legal requirement.

The legal analysis must connect the documented reasons and procedures with the relevant legal standard.

In Bridges, the Court of Appeal examined the lack of sufficiently clear criteria governing where AFR Locate could be deployed.

This demonstrates that evidence of operational authorisation and evidence of adequate legal constraints are not necessarily the same.

Evidence About Model Performance

Suppose the institution claims that its facial recognition system is reliable.

What evidence would support that claim?

A meaningful technical evaluation should identify the system or algorithm tested, the task performed, the test conditions, and the performance measures used.

The lawyer may need to know whether the evaluation concerned one-to-one verification or one-to-many identification.

They may need to know whether it used the same software version as the deployed system.

They may need to know the size and characteristics of the reference database.

They may need to know the threshold or candidate-selection procedure.

They may also need to understand which errors were counted and how the reported rates were calculated.

These questions follow from the statistical concepts examined in Section 1.4.

A claim of 99 per cent accuracy cannot be evaluated adequately without understanding what was measured.

A performance report may be scientifically sound for one purpose while providing limited evidence for another.

For example, a benchmark conducted on controlled photographs may not establish performance under all conditions encountered during live public surveillance.

The report may remain useful.

But its relevance must be assessed in relation to the deployment being examined.

Why the Exact Software Version Matters

Software changes over time.

Developers may correct defects, modify processing procedures, update models, or change the way results are displayed.

A software version identifies a particular release or state of a software product.

A model version identifies a particular state of the model used within the system.

These distinctions can matter because two versions of a product may behave differently.

Suppose a supplier produces a performance report for Model Version B.

The disputed event occurred while Model Version A was deployed.

The report may not establish the performance of Version A.

It may provide background information, but further evidence would be needed to determine whether the results apply.

The same issue arises with configuration settings.

A model may remain unchanged while its alert threshold is altered.

The resulting operational behaviour may change even though the underlying model version is identical.

A credible technical reconstruction should therefore identify both the relevant software or model version and the configuration used at the material time, where that information is necessary and available.

This is one reason why version records and configuration histories can be important sources of evidence.

What Is a Configuration?

A configuration is the set of settings that determines how a system operates within the capabilities provided by its software and hardware.

In facial recognition, configuration may include the threshold used to generate alerts, the reference database selected for a search, and other operational parameters.

A parameter is a value that influences a process.

We must distinguish operational configuration parameters from model parameters learned during training.

A model's learned parameters are part of the computational structure developed through training.

A deployment threshold may instead be selected or adjusted when the system is configured for use.

The two can influence results in different ways.

A lawyer examining a disputed alert may not need every learned parameter of the model.

But the threshold applied to the disputed comparison could be directly relevant.

The appropriate evidence depends on the proposition under examination.

A general request for all technical information may therefore be less useful than a carefully formulated request identifying the relevant configuration and time period.

Evidence About Human Review

A facial recognition alert may be followed by human review.

An institution may describe this as a safeguard.

But what evidence would establish that the safeguard operated effectively?

A written procedure may require operators to compare the images before accepting a match.

Training materials may explain how operators were instructed to perform that task.

Interface documentation may show what information was available.

Operational records may indicate whether a particular alert was accepted, rejected, or escalated.

A review record may include the operator's reasons.

These forms of evidence address different aspects of oversight.

The procedure establishes the intended responsibility.

The training materials establish what guidance was provided.

The interface documentation establishes what information the system was designed to display.

The operational record may show what action the reviewer took.

The reasons, if recorded, may help explain why the action was taken.

Even a complete record of these matters may not reveal every aspect of a person's mental process.

A click recorded in a log does not prove that the reviewer carefully considered the images.

Conversely, the absence of written reasons does not necessarily prove that no genuine review occurred.

The lawyer must avoid both assumptions.

Human oversight is an activity whose existence and effectiveness may require evidence beyond a policy statement.

Evidence About Demographic Performance

The Public Sector Equality Duty ground in Bridges demonstrates why evidence about demographic performance may matter.

A facial recognition system may perform differently across groups.

The relevant inquiry may concern whether the deploying authority investigated that possibility adequately.

A lawyer examining compliance with an equality-related duty might seek records of the authority's assessment of the risk.

These could include technical evaluations, correspondence with the supplier, requests for demographic performance information, internal discussions, and records of decisions about further testing.

The precise documents needed depend on the legal duty and the circumstances.

The existence of a demographic performance report would not necessarily prove compliance.

The report might concern a different system, dataset, or operational environment.

Nor would the absence of a documented performance difference necessarily establish that the system had no such difference.

The court in Bridges emphasised that the Public Sector Equality Duty required reasonable inquiry into relevant risks.

It did not find that the particular software had been proved to discriminate on grounds of race or sex.

This distinction affects the evidence required.

If the legal question is whether an authority made reasonable inquiries, evidence about the inquiries themselves may be central.

If the question is whether a particular individual suffered unlawful discriminatory treatment, additional evidence concerning the relevant outcome and applicable legal test may be required.

The lawyer must not substitute one inquiry for the other.

What Does an Independent Test Establish?

A supplier may test its own product.

An independent laboratory may also evaluate the system.

Independent testing can provide important evidence because it may reduce certain conflicts of interest and introduce structured evaluation procedures.

But independence does not eliminate the need to examine methodology.

A test may be independent yet unsuitable for the disputed question.

For example, an independent evaluation of one-to-one verification does not automatically establish the performance of a one-to-many watchlist search.

A test conducted on one software version may not describe another.

A test performed under laboratory conditions may not reproduce the relevant deployment environment.

The credibility of the evaluator and the relevance of the evaluation are separate considerations.

The lawyer should therefore examine both.

Who conducted the test?

What was tested?

How was the test designed?

What data were used?

Which results were obtained?

What limitations were acknowledged?

And what proposition is the report being used to support?

These questions allow technical evidence to be evaluated without assuming that every authoritative report answers every relevant question.

The Difference Between a Benchmark and an Incident Investigation

A benchmark is a structured evaluation used to compare or measure performance under defined conditions.

An incident investigation examines a particular event or failure.

The distinction is important.

A benchmark may show that a facial recognition algorithm performs well across thousands of test images.

An incident investigation may ask why the system generated an incorrect alert for one individual on one occasion.

The benchmark provides information about general performance under the test conditions.

The incident investigation concerns the facts of a particular event.

The two forms of evidence can inform one another.

For example, a benchmark may help an expert understand whether a particular type of error is consistent with known performance limitations.

But it does not, by itself, establish what caused the individual error.

A particular incorrect match might arise from poor image quality, a similarity-scoring error, a configuration choice, a reference-data problem, or a human review mistake.

Determining the cause requires evidence about the actual process.

This distinction is essential whenever general scientific research is introduced into litigation concerning a specific AI-assisted decision.

The Importance of Causation

A system may contain a defect.

A person may suffer harm.

But the existence of both facts does not automatically establish that the defect caused the harm.

Causation concerns the relationship between events, conduct, and consequences.

Its precise legal meaning depends on the cause of action and the applicable legal tests.

In an AI-assisted decision, causal analysis may require reconstruction of several stages.

Did the system generate an incorrect result?

Did the human reviewer accept that result?

Did the institution rely on it?

Did that reliance contribute to the action affecting the person?

Would the same action have occurred on other grounds?

These questions may be difficult to answer without operational records.

Consider a hypothetical case.

A facial recognition system generates an incorrect candidate.

The operator rejects it.

No police action follows.

The system made a technical error, but the error did not produce the hypothesised intervention.

Now consider a different case.

The system generates an incorrect candidate, the operator accepts it, and officers intervene solely because of the alert.

The causal sequence is different.

A legal claim may still require examination of authority, duties, harm, and other elements.

But reconstructing the sequence is a necessary starting point.

Technical error and legal causation should not be treated as identical.

What Is an Audit Trail?

An audit trail is a sequence of records that permits relevant events, decisions, or changes to be traced.

In an AI system, an audit trail may connect an input to a model output, a human review, and a subsequent action.

The value of an audit trail lies in its ability to support reconstruction.

For example, an investigator might need to establish which version of a model processed an application, what data were supplied, what score was returned, and whether a human decision-maker changed the recommended outcome.

A well-designed audit trail may make those questions easier to answer.

But the mere existence of logs does not guarantee a complete audit trail.

The records must be sufficiently connected, interpretable, and reliable for the purpose at hand.

An audit trail that records model outputs but not subsequent decisions may be unable to establish how the outputs affected people.

An audit trail that records final decisions but not the model versions used may be unable to reconstruct the technical basis of those decisions.

The evidential value depends on what can actually be traced.

Authenticity and Integrity: Can We Trust the Record?

Before relying on a digital record, an investigator may need to establish that it is authentic and has not been materially altered.

Authenticity concerns whether the evidence is what it is represented to be.

Integrity concerns whether information has remained complete and unaltered in the relevant respects.

These are related but distinct ideas.

A file may genuinely originate from an organisation yet have been modified after its creation.

A file may remain unchanged but be incorrectly described as representing a particular event.

Digital evidence may therefore require information about its origin, handling, and preservation.

One relevant concept is a cryptographic hash.

A cryptographic hash function takes digital data as input and produces a fixed-length value, often called a hash or digest.

For a suitable cryptographic hash function, even a small change in the input will ordinarily produce a different digest.

A hash can therefore help detect whether a file has changed between two points in time.

But a hash does not prove that the original contents were accurate.

Nor does it establish who created the file or whether the event described in it actually occurred.

It can support an integrity claim when used within an appropriate preservation and verification process.

This illustrates a broader principle: a technical safeguard must be interpreted according to what it actually demonstrates.

Chain of Custody: Following the Evidence Itself

A chain of custody is the documented history of how evidence has been collected, handled, transferred, stored, and preserved.

The concept is especially important when the authenticity or integrity of evidence may be disputed.

Suppose a lawyer obtains a copy of an operational log.

Who extracted it?

From which system?

When?

Using what method?

Was the original preserved?

Was the copy modified?

Who subsequently had access to it?

Answers to these questions may help establish the reliability of the evidence.

The precise procedural requirements depend on the jurisdiction and type of proceeding.

Not every digital document requires an identical preservation procedure.

But the underlying concern is consistent: the decision-maker needs a reliable basis for concluding that the evidence being examined corresponds to the relevant source or event.

Technical evidence does not become trustworthy merely because it is digital.

Its provenance and handling may matter.

What Is Provenance?

Provenance means the origin and history of an item of information or evidence.

For data used in AI systems, provenance may concern where the data came from, how they were collected, and what transformations they underwent.

For a digital record, provenance may concern which system generated it and how it reached the investigator.

For a model, provenance may include information about its development, training sources, versions, and deployment history.

The precise scope depends on the question.

Provenance is important because the meaning and reliability of information often depend on its history.

A photograph obtained from an official identification document may have different evidential characteristics from an image downloaded from an unknown online source.

A dataset assembled from verified records may differ from one containing unverified entries.

A performance report generated by an identified laboratory may be easier to evaluate than an unattributed screenshot displaying a percentage.

The relevant question is not whether one source is always reliable and another always unreliable.

It is what can be established about the information's origin, processing, and suitability for the intended use.

Evidence of Governance Is More Than a Policy

An institution may claim that it governs its AI systems responsibly.

What would support that claim?

A governance policy is a starting point.

But a policy describes an intended arrangement.

Evidence of implementation may require additional records.

These could include risk assessments, assigned responsibilities, training records, testing reports, approvals, monitoring results, incident investigations, and documented corrective actions.

The precise evidence should correspond to the governance claim being made.

For example, if an organisation claims that model performance is monitored, relevant evidence might include monitoring procedures and actual monitoring results.

If it claims that employees can override automated recommendations, evidence might include the interface design, relevant policies, and records showing whether overrides occur.

If it claims that demographic performance is evaluated, evidence might include the evaluation methodology, results, and records of how findings were considered.

A governance claim becomes more credible when it can be connected to verifiable activities.

This principle is consistent with the emphasis on documentation, measurement, and risk management in the NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework.

However, the NIST AI RMF is a voluntary framework. Its recommendations should not automatically be presented as binding legal obligations in every jurisdiction.

The applicable legal requirements must be identified separately.

An evidence map connects a proposition requiring examination with the categories of information that may help establish or challenge it.

Identify the system activity relevant to that proposition.

Determine what records or tests could provide evidence.

Then evaluate what those materials actually establish.

When Evidence Is Unavailable

AI systems may be difficult to investigate because relevant information is missing, inaccessible, or controlled by different organisations.

A supplier may regard certain technical information as confidential.

An institution may not retain particular operational records.

A system may delete information automatically.

A model may be too complex for its internal operations to be explained through a simple rule.

These difficulties are real.

But they do not justify inventing conclusions.

If the relevant evidence is unavailable, the lawyer must identify the resulting uncertainty.

Depending on the applicable law and procedure, the absence of evidence may have different consequences.

It may limit the ability to prove a factual allegation.

It may raise questions about whether an institution complied with documentation or record-keeping duties.

It may affect the weight assigned to an unsupported assertion.

In some legal settings, particular failures to preserve or produce evidence may have procedural consequences.

Those consequences cannot be assumed universally.

They depend on the relevant legal framework and circumstances.

The important discipline is to distinguish three situations.

The first is where evidence supports a proposition.

The second is where evidence contradicts it.

The third is where the available evidence is insufficient to determine the answer.

The third situation is not automatically equivalent to either of the first two.

What the Evidence in Bridges Could—and Could Not—Establish

The Bridges litigation illustrates how legal questions can be resolved through different kinds of evidence.

The court examined the legal and policy framework governing AFR Locate.

It considered the categories of people who could be included on watchlists.

It examined the discretion governing deployment locations.

It considered the content and legal adequacy of the data protection impact assessment.

It also examined the steps taken by South Wales Police in relation to the possibility of demographic differences in the software's performance.

These matters were central to the successful grounds of appeal.

But the judgment should not be treated as though it contained a complete technical audit of the facial recognition model.

The court did not establish the full internal architecture of the proprietary software.

It did not determine that the system was actually biased against particular demographic groups.

It did not establish that Edward Bridges's own face had certainly been processed.

And it did not convert general research about facial recognition errors into a case-specific finding that every disputed alert was incorrect.

The judgment's importance lies in the legal conclusions supported by the evidence before the court.

That is precisely the distinction this section seeks to teach.

A sound legal argument is not one that collects the largest possible quantity of technical material.

It is one that connects relevant evidence to the propositions that matter.

From Evidence Collection to an Assurance Case

The evidential method developed here has an application beyond litigation.

It can also support responsible governance before a dispute arises.

Suppose an organisation wishes to demonstrate that its AI-assisted decision process is appropriately controlled.

It might begin with a claim:

“The system is subject to effective human oversight.”

That claim requires an explanation.

What does effective oversight mean in this context?

Who performs it?

What information do reviewers receive?

What authority do they possess?

What happens when they disagree with the system?

The organisation must then identify evidence supporting its answers.

That evidence might include operating procedures, interface documentation, training records, monitoring results, and records of actual reviews.

The structure can be described as:

Claim → Reasoned justification → Supporting evidence.

This resembles the logic of an assurance case.

An assurance case is a structured argument supported by evidence intended to justify confidence in a particular claim about a system.

Assurance cases have a history in safety-critical engineering and have increasingly been discussed in relation to AI governance.

They are not equivalent to a judicial determination.

Nor does producing an assurance case automatically establish legal compliance.

Their value lies in requiring an organisation to make its claims explicit, explain why the supporting evidence is relevant, and identify the limits of the justification.

Later in this book, we will develop a legal–technical assurance method in greater detail.

For now, the essential connection is clear.

The same disciplined approach that helps a lawyer challenge an unsupported technical claim can help an institution avoid making unsupported claims in the first place.

The Difference Between Knowing and Demonstrating

An engineer may believe that a model performs reliably.

A manager may believe that the organisation has adequate safeguards.

A lawyer may believe that a system was used unlawfully.

Each belief may be reasonable.

But professional analysis requires a further step.

What can be demonstrated?

The engineer may need evaluation results.

The manager may need operational records.

The lawyer may need evidence connecting the relevant conduct with an applicable legal rule.

This is the difference between an assertion and an evidenced conclusion.

It does not require absolute certainty about every aspect of a system.

Many legal and technical assessments operate under uncertainty.

But uncertainty must be identified, not concealed.

A credible analysis states what is known, what remains unknown, and how those limits affect the conclusion.

Returning to the Person Before the Camera

We began this chapter with a person passing through a public place.

A camera captured visual information.

Software may have processed that information into a biometric representation.

A comparison may have produced a score.

A threshold may have generated an alert.

A human operator may have reviewed it.

Police officers may then have acted.

Each stage presents potential questions of fact, engineering, and law.

But the questions cannot all be answered by the same evidence.

A camera record may establish that an image was captured.

A technical log may establish that a comparison occurred.

A performance report may describe how a system behaved under specified tests.

A policy may identify the rules governing its use.

A review record may show what an operator did.

A legal instrument may establish the authority or duty applicable to the institution.

A judicial decision may determine how the law applies to established facts.

These materials perform different evidential functions.

Their value depends on their relevance, reliability, and the conclusions drawn from them.

The lawyer's task is not to treat technology as an unknowable black box.

Nor is it to assume that every technical detail must be mastered personally.

The task is to ask sufficiently precise questions to identify what must be established and what expertise or evidence is needed to establish it.

This is the foundation of evidence-based AI law.

We have now learned how to move from a legal concern to a structured inquiry into the technological system.

But an important question remains.

Even when technical evidence is available, how far can it take us?

Can a performance test prove that a particular decision was lawful?

Can a model explanation establish causation?

Can the absence of recorded errors establish that a system is safe?

And can technical reliability ever substitute for legal justification?

These questions concern the limits of technical proof.

They are the subject of the next section.

1.8 The Limits of Technical Proof

When the Machine Is Right, Is the Decision Lawful?

Imagine that a facial recognition system identifies a person correctly.

The camera captures a clear image. The software detects the face without difficulty. The model produces a strong similarity score. The operator examines the images and confirms that they depict the same individual.

There is no apparent identification error.

Does that mean the system was used lawfully?

Not necessarily.

The technology may have performed its intended task correctly while the institution operating it failed to satisfy a legal requirement.

Perhaps the person's photograph was included in the reference database without an adequate legal basis.

Perhaps the surveillance operation was conducted under rules that failed to constrain official discretion.

Perhaps the institution had not properly assessed the privacy risks associated with processing biometric information.

Or perhaps it had failed to investigate a foreseeable risk of differential performance across demographic groups.

None of these questions can be answered simply by demonstrating that two photographs depict the same person.

This reveals a fundamental distinction:

Technical correctness and legal justification are different propositions.

The first concerns whether a technological process produced a result consistent with its defined task.

The second concerns whether the relevant conduct satisfied the applicable legal requirements.

The propositions may be connected. But evidence establishing one does not automatically establish the other.

This distinction lies at the heart of evidence-based AI law.

What Does Technical Proof Actually Establish?

The expression technical proof requires some care.

In mathematics, a proof may establish a proposition deductively from accepted premises.

In empirical science and engineering, conclusions are generally supported through observation, measurement, experimentation, and analysis.

A performance evaluation does not ordinarily establish that a system will behave correctly in every possible circumstance.

It provides evidence about performance under defined conditions.

Suppose a laboratory tests a facial recognition algorithm using a specified collection of images.

The evaluation reports the number of correct identifications and the number of errors.

What has been established?

Subject to the reliability of the testing process, the results provide evidence about how the algorithm performed on those test cases.

The evaluation may also support conclusions about expected performance under sufficiently comparable conditions.

But the evidence has limits.

It does not automatically establish that the algorithm will perform identically with every camera, population, image quality, or operational configuration.

It does not prove that every future identification will be correct.

And it does not establish that the institution using the algorithm possesses lawful authority to process the relevant information.

A technical test answers the questions it was designed to investigate.

Its conclusions should not be extended beyond the evidence without justification.

The Difference Between Verification and Validation

Two engineering concepts help explain this limitation: verification and validation.

Although their precise definitions vary across standards and disciplines, the concepts are commonly distinguished as follows.

Verification examines whether a system or component satisfies specified requirements.

Validation examines whether the system is suitable for its intended use.

The difference can be illustrated through a hypothetical public-benefits system.

Suppose a public authority develops software to calculate whether applicants satisfy an income threshold.

The technical specification requires the software to reject applications where the recorded income exceeds a defined amount.

Engineers test the software and establish that it applies the programmed rule correctly.

That is evidence relevant to verification.

But suppose the underlying eligibility rule is legally incorrect, or the recorded income data are unsuitable for determining eligibility.

The software may implement its specification perfectly while remaining unsuitable for the institution's lawful purpose.

That raises questions of validation and legal compliance.

The same distinction applies to facial recognition.

An algorithm may correctly implement a specified comparison procedure.

But the institution must still establish whether that procedure is appropriate for the intended operational environment.

And even a system validated for its intended technical purpose must be used within the applicable legal framework.

Verification, validation, and legal compliance are related inquiries.

They are not synonyms.

The First Boundary: From Statistical Performance to Individual Identity

Consider a facial recognition system that performs well in a large independent evaluation.

Its reported error rates are low under the tested conditions.

Now suppose the system generates a possible match involving a particular person.

Can the general performance report prove that this specific identification is correct?

No.

It may provide relevant information about the reliability of the recognition method.

But the correctness of the particular identification remains a separate factual question.

A system with a low error rate can still make an error in an individual case.

Similarly, the fact that a system sometimes makes errors does not establish that a particular result was incorrect.

This is the distinction between population-level evidence and case-specific evidence.

Population-level evidence describes patterns or performance across a defined group of observations.

Case-specific evidence concerns the particular event being examined.

Both can be relevant.

But they support different conclusions.

For example, an independent benchmark may help an expert evaluate the expected reliability of a recognition algorithm.

The actual images, system records, and circumstances of a disputed alert may help establish whether the particular identification was correct.

Neither category automatically replaces the other.

The Second Boundary: From Identity to Wrongdoing

Suppose the facial recognition system correctly identifies a person whose photograph appears on a police watchlist.

What has the system established?

At most, the recognition result may support the proposition that the captured face corresponds to a particular enrolled identity.

It has not established that the person committed a crime.

It has not established that the grounds for including the person on the watchlist were accurate.

It has not established that the person may lawfully be arrested.

These are separate propositions.

A person may appear on a watchlist for different reasons.

The legal significance of their inclusion depends on the applicable rules and the underlying facts.

The system's ability to identify the person does not determine the validity of those reasons.

This is an example of a broader problem in AI-assisted decision-making.

A model may be trained to predict one thing, while an institution treats its output as evidence of something else.

A model that predicts a statistical association does not necessarily establish causation.

A model that estimates risk does not establish that an individual has committed wrongdoing.

A model that classifies a document does not establish the legal truth of every statement within it.

The lawyer must identify the exact proposition the system was designed to address.

Then the lawyer must examine whether the institution has drawn a conclusion that exceeds that proposition.

The Third Boundary: From Technical Reliability to Legal Authority

We can now return to Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Article 8(1), incorporated into UK law through Schedule 1 to the Human Rights Act 1998, protects the right to respect for private and family life, home, and correspondence.

Article 8(2) permits certain interferences by public authorities only where the applicable requirements are satisfied.

The provision requires, among other matters, that the interference be in accordance with the law and necessary in a democratic society for a permitted purpose.

These are legal conditions.

A facial recognition accuracy report cannot establish them by itself.

The report may help assess whether the technology is suitable for a particular purpose or whether its risks are proportionate to its benefits.

But the existence and quality of the legal framework must be examined separately.

This was central to Bridges.

The Court of Appeal accepted that South Wales Police possessed a general legal basis for using facial recognition technology.

However, it concluded that the governing arrangements did not adequately constrain important aspects of police discretion.

The deficiencies concerned who could be included on a watchlist and where AFR Locate could be deployed.

These questions were not resolved by demonstrating the technical performance of the facial recognition algorithm.

They concerned the quality of the legal framework governing the interference with Article 8 rights.

The distinction is fundamental.

A system's reliability may be relevant to its justification.

But reliability does not create legal authority where the necessary authority or safeguards are absent.

Reading Article 8 as an Engineer and a Lawyer

Consider the phrase in accordance with the law.

For the lawyer, it raises questions about the legal basis, accessibility, foreseeability, and safeguards governing an interference.

For the engineer, the phrase does not directly specify a mathematical test.

There is no universal algorithm that can calculate whether a surveillance operation is legally authorised.

Nevertheless, engineering evidence may help answer factual questions relevant to the legal inquiry.

For example, system documentation may establish which individuals can be selected for identification.

Configuration records may show whether operational restrictions were technically enforced.

Deployment records may identify where and when the system operated.

Access-control records may indicate which personnel were permitted to modify the watchlist.

These materials do not themselves determine whether Article 8 was satisfied.

They provide evidence about the system and its operation that may be relevant to applying Article 8.

The legal standard identifies what matters.

The technical investigation helps establish the relevant facts.

The final legal interpretation requires legal reasoning.

This is the relationship we must preserve throughout the book.

The Fourth Boundary: From a Risk Assessment to Actual Compliance

Section 64 of the Data Protection Act 2018 concerns data protection impact assessments in the law-enforcement processing regime.

Under section 64(1), where a type of processing is likely to create a high risk to individuals' rights and freedoms, the controller must carry out a data protection impact assessment before the processing.

Section 64(3) specifies matters that the assessment must include.

These include a general description of the envisaged processing, an assessment of risks, measures intended to address those risks, and relevant safeguards.

The statutory structure is important.

The law does not merely ask whether the organisation possesses a document labelled DPIA.

It specifies substantive matters that the assessment must address.

Now consider a hypothetical institution that produces a DPIA stating:

“The facial recognition system has been tested and is accurate.”

That statement may be relevant.

But does it adequately assess the risks to individuals' rights and freedoms?

Not necessarily.

The assessment may need to consider risks beyond incorrect identification.

These could include the scope of biometric processing, the categories of people affected, the purposes of use, the safeguards governing deployment, and the consequences of the processing.

The legal adequacy of the assessment depends on the statutory requirements and the circumstances.

A technical performance report may therefore contribute to a DPIA without replacing it.

The Court of Appeal's conclusion in Bridges illustrates the distinction.

It found that the DPIA did not comply with the requirements of section 64(3)(b) and (c).

Those provisions concern the assessment of risks and the measures envisaged to address them.

The deficiency was not simply that the court had discovered a particular incorrect facial match.

It concerned the adequacy of the institution's assessment of the relevant risks and safeguards.

A Historical Legal Qualification

The statutory provisions discussed in Bridges must be understood in their historical context.

The Court of Appeal delivered its judgment on 11 August 2020.

The relevant data protection regime included Part 3 of the Data Protection Act 2018, as applicable to the law-enforcement processing under examination.

Subsequent amendments to UK data protection legislation do not automatically describe the law as it stood during the disputed deployments or at the time of judgment.

Accordingly, when analysing the case, the legally relevant historical text must be distinguished from later legislative developments.

This is not merely a technical point about citation.

It is part of accurate legal reasoning.

A legal conclusion about past conduct must be assessed against the law applicable to that conduct, subject to the relevant rules governing temporal application.

A contemporary compliance assessment may require examination of the legislation currently in force.

The two inquiries should not be merged.

The Fifth Boundary: From the Absence of Proven Bias to Compliance with Equality Duties

Section 149(1) of the Equality Act 2010 establishes the Public Sector Equality Duty.

The provision requires public authorities, when exercising their functions, to have due regard to specified equality objectives.

These include eliminating prohibited discrimination, advancing equality of opportunity, and fostering good relations between relevant groups.

The duty does not require a public authority to guarantee that every technological system will produce identical outcomes for all demographic groups.

Nor does it automatically prohibit the use of a system whenever a potential risk is identified.

But it requires the authority to give appropriate consideration to relevant equality matters.

In Bridges, the Court of Appeal examined whether South Wales Police had taken reasonable steps to investigate the possibility that the facial recognition software might operate with differential accuracy according to race or sex.

The court concluded that the police had failed to discharge the duty.

This is a particularly instructive example of the limits of technical proof.

The court did not establish that AFR Locate had actually discriminated against a particular demographic group.

Instead, it found that the authority had failed to conduct the inquiry required by its legal duty.

The difference is decisive.

A technical study demonstrating differential error rates could be relevant to a substantive discrimination claim.

Evidence showing what inquiries the authority undertook could be relevant to compliance with the Public Sector Equality Duty.

The two categories of evidence may overlap, but they answer different questions.

A failure to prove that a system is biased is not equivalent to proof that the authority adequately investigated the possibility of bias.

When a Legal Duty Requires Inquiry Rather Than Certainty

This distinction reveals something important about law.

Legal obligations do not always require institutions to guarantee perfect outcomes.

Sometimes the obligation concerns the quality of the decision-making process.

An institution may be required to identify relevant risks, obtain appropriate information, consider available evidence, and make a reasoned decision.

Whether those steps are legally sufficient depends on the applicable duty.

The Public Sector Equality Duty provides one example.

The authority must give due regard to the relevant equality objectives.

In some circumstances, that requires investigation of matters about which the authority does not yet possess sufficient information.

A public authority cannot necessarily satisfy the duty by asserting that no adverse effect has been demonstrated.

That assertion may reflect a lack of investigation rather than a reasoned conclusion.

For an AI system, the distinction creates an important evidential question:

What did the institution do to understand the risks before relying on the technology?

The answer may be found in correspondence with suppliers, evaluation reports, internal assessments, records of decisions, or other relevant material.

A model's numerical accuracy cannot establish the adequacy of that institutional process on its own.

The Sixth Boundary: From Explanation to Causation

Suppose an AI system rejects an application.

The applicant asks why.

The institution produces an explanation stating that the model assigned substantial importance to a particular feature.

Does that explanation establish why the applicant was rejected?

It may provide relevant information.

But the answer depends on what kind of explanation was produced and what proposition it is being used to support.

Some explanation methods describe relationships between model inputs and outputs.

Others approximate a model's behaviour around a particular prediction.

Some identify features that are associated with changes in the output under specified assumptions.

These methods do not all establish causal relationships.

A feature may be statistically influential within a model without being the real-world cause of the outcome being investigated.

For example, suppose a lending model assigns a high risk score partly because an applicant lives in a particular area.

That observation does not establish that the geographical location itself causes financial default.

The model may have learned a statistical association.

The association may reflect other variables, historical patterns, or relationships within the training data.

Whether the feature is legally permissible, discriminatory, causally relevant, or appropriate for the intended purpose requires further analysis.

A technical explanation is therefore evidence about the system's behaviour.

It is not automatically an explanation of every causal or legal question surrounding the decision.

Correlation Is Not Causation

The distinction between correlation and causation is one of the foundations of responsible data analysis.

Correlation concerns a statistical relationship between variables.

Causation concerns whether a change in one factor produces a change in another under the relevant causal interpretation.

The concepts are not identical.

Suppose a model learns that a particular characteristic is associated with a higher probability of an adverse outcome.

That relationship may be useful for prediction.

But it does not establish that the characteristic causes the outcome.

Nor does it determine whether using the characteristic is lawful.

A model can be predictively accurate while relying on variables that raise legal or ethical concerns.

Conversely, a variable may have a legitimate causal relationship with an outcome but be measured inaccurately or used in an inappropriate decision-making process.

The lawyer must therefore distinguish the model's predictive function from the causal and normative questions raised by its use.

This issue will become especially important when we examine discrimination, lending, employment, and automated public administration.

The Seventh Boundary: From a Correct Output to a Fair Process

Imagine a public authority using an automated system to identify applications requiring additional scrutiny.

The system correctly flags an application according to its programmed criteria.

The authority then refuses the application without allowing the applicant to correct inaccurate information or present relevant circumstances.

The model may have operated correctly.

But that does not determine whether the institution's procedure complied with the applicable legal requirements.

Depending on the jurisdiction and decision, relevant legal rules may concern fairness, reasons, participation, review, or opportunities to challenge an outcome.

These protections do not arise identically in every legal system.

Their existence and scope must be established from the relevant law.

The general analytical point remains:

The correctness of a computational output does not automatically establish the fairness or lawfulness of the procedure through which it is used.

A system can produce accurate information within an inadequate decision-making process.

A lawful process may require more than accurate information.

The Eighth Boundary: From Documentation to Reality

An institution may maintain detailed policies governing AI use.

The policies may require human oversight, regular testing, and documented review.

These are potentially important safeguards.

But written requirements do not prove that the corresponding activities occurred.

Suppose a policy states that every facial recognition alert must be reviewed by a trained officer.

To evaluate compliance, an investigator may need evidence of the training provided, the interface available to the officer, the actual review performed, and the records retained.

The policy establishes the intended rule.

Operational evidence helps establish its implementation.

This is the difference between documented compliance and verifiable compliance.

A technically detailed governance document can still fail to describe what happened in practice.

Conversely, the absence of a particular document does not necessarily prove that no safeguard operated, although missing documentation may itself have legal significance where record-keeping duties apply.

The evidential conclusion must follow from the available facts and applicable legal requirements.

Every evidential claim has a scope.

An expert report may be authoritative within that scope.

A legal error occurs when the report is treated as proving something beyond it.

The Role of Expert Evidence

Technical experts can assist legal decision-makers in understanding matters outside ordinary experience.

An expert may explain how a facial recognition algorithm operates.

They may evaluate the methodology of a performance report.

They may examine whether a particular image was suitable for comparison.

They may assess the significance of an error rate.

But technical expertise does not automatically confer authority to decide legal questions.

An expert's role depends on the applicable procedural rules and the issues on which their expertise is relevant.

For example, an expert may be able to explain whether a model's reported similarity score represents a calibrated probability.

The court must still determine the legal significance of the evidence.

An expert may identify weaknesses in an institution's technical evaluation.

The court must still apply the relevant statutory duties and legal standards.

This division of responsibilities protects both technical and legal reasoning.

The lawyer should not replace scientific analysis with unsupported intuition.

The expert should not be treated as the final authority on questions of law merely because the underlying technology is complex.

The strongest analysis respects the boundaries of both disciplines.

A Court Does Not Need to Understand Every Line of Code

One might assume that a court cannot evaluate an AI-assisted decision without examining the complete source code.

That assumption is too broad.

Source code can be relevant, and in some disputes it may be important.

But different legal questions require different evidence.

In Bridges, the Court of Appeal reached conclusions about the adequacy of the legal framework, the DPIA, and compliance with the Public Sector Equality Duty.

Those conclusions did not require the court to establish every internal mathematical detail of the facial recognition model.

The court examined the evidence relevant to the legal issues before it.

This demonstrates an important methodological lesson.

The objective of legal–technical analysis is not to reconstruct every component of a system regardless of relevance.

It is to understand the system sufficiently to identify and evaluate the evidence needed for the particular legal question.

A request for source code may be justified in one case and unnecessary in another.

A request for policies, testing records, or operational logs may be more directly relevant to a different issue.

The choice should follow from the proposition requiring proof.

When Technical Evidence Is Insufficient

Sometimes the available evidence cannot support a firm conclusion.

A system may have been retired.

Operational logs may have been deleted.

The relevant model version may no longer be available.

The supplier may be unable to reproduce the original configuration.

The underlying images may be missing.

These circumstances can limit the conclusions that an expert or lawyer can responsibly draw.

It is important to distinguish three statements.

The first is:

“The evidence establishes that the system produced an incorrect result.”

The second is:

“The evidence establishes that the system produced a correct result.”

The third is:

“The available evidence is insufficient to determine whether the result was correct.”

The third statement is not equivalent to either of the first two.

It identifies an evidential limitation.

Depending on the applicable law, the limitation may affect the ability to discharge a burden of proof or demonstrate compliance with a duty.

But the consequences must be determined through legal analysis.

Technical uncertainty should be described accurately rather than converted into an unsupported conclusion.

What Bridges Ultimately Demonstrates

The significance of Bridges is not that the Court of Appeal proved facial recognition technology to be inherently unreliable.

It did not.

Nor did the court conclude that every use of facial recognition by police forces was necessarily unlawful.

Its conclusions were more specific.

The legal framework governing the deployment failed to provide adequate constraints on important areas of police discretion.

The DPIA did not satisfy the statutory requirements identified by the court.

And South Wales Police had failed to discharge the Public Sector Equality Duty.

The court rejected the separate proportionality challenge and the other data protection ground.

The outcome demonstrates that legal compliance cannot be inferred from technical performance alone.

Even a highly reliable identification system operates within an institutional and legal framework.

The adequacy of that framework is a separate matter requiring separate evidence.

The Principle of Evidential Fit

We can now formulate a principle that will guide the remainder of this book.

Evidence must fit the proposition it is offered to establish.

A model-performance report should not be treated as proof of legal authority.

A policy should not be treated as proof of operational compliance.

A similarity score should not be treated as a finding of guilt.

A model explanation should not automatically be treated as a causal explanation.

And the absence of demonstrated discrimination should not automatically be treated as proof that an institution fulfilled a duty to investigate discrimination risks.

Each proposition requires evidence appropriate to its nature.

This principle may appear simple.

Its practical implications are substantial.

It determines which documents a lawyer should request, which questions an expert should answer, which tests an institution should conduct, and which conclusions a court can reasonably draw.

It also provides the foundation for a more rigorous approach to AI governance.

Instead of asking whether an organisation possesses a large collection of compliance documents, we can ask whether its important claims are supported by relevant, reliable, and sufficient evidence.

The Final Question Before Our Method Begins

We began with a person walking through a public street.

A camera captured their image.

Software transformed visual information into numerical representations.

A model compared those representations with a watchlist.

A threshold determined whether an alert should be generated.

A human operator reviewed the result.

An institution could then take action.

At every stage, different questions arose.

Some were questions of engineering.

Some were questions of statistics.

Some were questions of institutional procedure.

Others were questions of law.

The stages were connected, but their evidential requirements were not identical.

The most important lesson is therefore not that lawyers must become software engineers.

It is that lawyers must learn to recognise the boundaries of technical evidence.

They must understand what a system measures, what its outputs mean, what its tests establish, and where additional factual or legal analysis is required.

Likewise, engineers working on consequential AI systems must understand that technical success does not automatically establish lawful or responsible use.

Both professions need a method for connecting their questions.

The next section develops that method.

It will show how to begin with a legal concern, reconstruct the relevant system, identify the evidence required, evaluate the technical findings, and return to the legal question without confusing the different stages.

The purpose is not to make every lawyer an engineer or every engineer a lawyer.

It is to make their reasoning capable of meeting at the evidence.

CHAPTER ONE

1.9 A Method for Asking Better Questions

The Lawyer Who Asked for the Algorithm

Imagine a lawyer entering a meeting with the technical team of a company.

The company has introduced an artificial intelligence system to assist with decisions about customers. A client believes that the system contributed to an unfair decision.

The lawyer wants to investigate.

They begin with a question:

“Can you show me the algorithm?”

The engineer hesitates.

Which algorithm?

The software contains several components. One prepares the data. Another calculates a prediction. A third applies a decision threshold. A separate component displays the result to employees.

The system also interacts with a database and a set of institutional rules.

The lawyer repeats the request.

“I need to understand how the AI made its decision.”

The engineer begins describing the model architecture.

They explain the training procedure, the mathematical functions, and the software libraries used during development.

After twenty minutes, the lawyer has learned several technical terms.

But the central legal question remains unanswered.

Why was the client's application rejected?

The engineer has explained how the model produces predictions.

The lawyer needs to establish how a particular prediction contributed to a particular institutional decision.

These are different inquiries.

The difficulty is not necessarily that the lawyer lacks technical intelligence or that the engineer lacks legal awareness.

The difficulty is that they have begun at different points.

The lawyer is thinking about a legally significant outcome.

The engineer is thinking about the computational component.

Neither has yet reconstructed the relationship between them.

A more productive conversation would begin with a different question:

What exactly must we establish about this decision, and which part of the system could provide the relevant evidence?

This question changes the investigation.

It begins with the proposition requiring examination rather than the technology that happens to be involved.

It also reveals the central method developed throughout this chapter.

The objective is not to collect every technical detail.

It is to connect legal questions with the technical and institutional evidence capable of answering them.

From Two Professional Languages to One Investigation

Law and engineering approach problems through different forms of reasoning.

A lawyer may begin by identifying a right, duty, legal power, or disputed fact.

An engineer may begin by identifying a system requirement, computational process, performance measure, or failure condition.

Both approaches can be rigorous.

But they do not necessarily use the same vocabulary.

Consider the expression fairness.

A lawyer may use it to discuss procedural fairness, discrimination, or the requirements of a particular legal regime.

An engineer may use it to describe a statistical relationship between model outcomes across groups.

These meanings may overlap.

They are not identical.

A statistical fairness measure cannot automatically determine whether conduct constitutes unlawful discrimination.

Equally, a legal prohibition on discrimination does not automatically specify one mathematical metric that every model must satisfy.

The relationship must be constructed through careful interpretation.

Consider another expression: accuracy.

An engineer may understand accuracy as the proportion of correct classifications within an evaluation dataset.

A lawyer may ask whether the information relied upon in a particular decision was factually accurate.

The first is a performance measure.

The second concerns the reliability of evidence in a particular case.

Again, the questions are connected but distinct.

Effective legal–technical analysis requires both professions to identify the meaning of the terms they use.

The lawyer must understand what the technical measurement establishes.

The engineer must understand what legal proposition the measurement is intended to support.

Neither profession should assume that a familiar word carries an identical meaning in the other discipline.

A Six-Stage Method for Legal–Technical AI Assurance

We can now introduce the central analytical method of this book.

It consists of six connected stages:

Legal Question → System Reconstruction → Evidence Requirements → Technical Evaluation → Legal Interpretation → Assurance or Remedy

The sequence provides a structured way to investigate AI-assisted decisions and evaluate claims about their governance.

It is proposed here as a practical legal–technical methodology.

It is not a statutory test established by the Court of Appeal in Bridges, nor is it presented as an official NIST framework.

Rather, it draws on established principles of legal reasoning, systems engineering, technical evaluation, and evidence-based assurance.

Each stage answers a different question.

The first identifies what the law requires or what factual proposition is disputed.

The second establishes how the relevant system operates.

The third identifies the evidence needed to examine the proposition.

The fourth evaluates the technical evidence using appropriate methods.

The fifth interprets the findings within the applicable legal framework.

The sixth determines what conclusion, assurance, correction, or remedy can responsibly follow.

The stages are logically connected.

They are not necessarily performed only once.

An investigation may reveal missing information that requires returning to an earlier stage.

A technical finding may expose a legal issue that was not initially apparent.

A legal interpretation may require additional testing.

The method is therefore structured but iterative.

Its purpose is to preserve the relationship between the question, the evidence, and the conclusion.

Stage One: Legal Question

What Exactly Are We Trying to Establish?

Every investigation should begin with a sufficiently precise question.

Suppose a public authority uses facial recognition technology.

A lawyer might initially ask:

“Is this AI system lawful?”

The question is understandable, but it is too broad to guide a technical investigation.

Lawfulness depends on the applicable jurisdiction, the relevant activity, the legal obligations engaged, and the circumstances of use.

A more useful inquiry separates the broad concern into specific propositions.

For example:

Does the processing interfere with a legally protected right?

What legal authority permits the processing?

Are the conditions governing the exercise of that authority sufficiently clear?

Has the institution satisfied the applicable data protection requirements?

Has it complied with any relevant equality duties?

These questions identify different legal issues.

They may require different evidence.

The first task is therefore to identify the applicable legal rule and the proposition that must be established.

Beginning with the Legal Text

Return to Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Article 8(1) provides:

“Everyone has the right to respect for his private and family life, his home and his correspondence.”

Article 8(2) establishes the conditions under which public authorities may interfere with that right.

For present purposes, the essential requirements include that the interference be in accordance with the law, pursue a permitted legitimate aim, and satisfy the applicable necessity standard.

The lawyer should not begin by asking the engineer whether the system is Article 8 compliant.

That would transfer a legal conclusion to someone whose expertise may concern only the technology.

Instead, the lawyer should identify factual questions relevant to applying Article 8.

What information does the system collect?

Does it process biometric information?

Whose information is processed?

For what purpose?

Who authorises the processing?

What restrictions govern the operation?

These are questions that technical and institutional evidence may help answer.

The legal rule establishes the direction of the inquiry.

The evidence establishes the relevant facts.

The legal conclusion follows only after those facts have been evaluated under the applicable standard.

The Legal Question Must Be Narrow Enough to Investigate

A question such as “Is the algorithm biased?” may also be too broad.

Bias can refer to several different phenomena.

It may concern differences in data representation, prediction errors, institutional selection practices, or treatment of legally protected groups.

Not every technical difference constitutes unlawful discrimination.

Not every legal equality duty requires proof of an actual discriminatory outcome.

In Bridges, the successful equality ground concerned compliance with section 149 of the Equality Act 2010.

The relevant question was whether South Wales Police had taken the steps required to investigate the possibility of differential accuracy in the facial recognition software.

That question is more precise than asking whether facial recognition technology is generally biased.

It directs attention towards the authority's knowledge, inquiries, and decision-making process.

The legal question therefore determines the evidential target.

The better the question, the more relevant the investigation can become.

Stage Two: System Reconstruction

What Actually Happens Between Input and Action?

Once the legal question has been identified, the next task is to reconstruct the relevant system.

This means establishing the components, information flows, decision rules, and human activities involved in producing the outcome.

It does not necessarily require examining every line of source code.

The required depth depends on the legal question.

Consider the facial recognition system in Bridges.

The Court of Appeal described the principal technical stages of AFR Locate in paragraph [9] of its judgment.

The process involved compiling or using a watchlist, acquiring facial images, detecting faces, extracting facial features, comparing those features, and generating possible matches according to a similarity threshold.

Paragraph [15] described the subsequent human review and possible police intervention.

Together, these passages provide a starting point for reconstructing the relevant system.

The sequence can be expressed as:

Watchlist preparation → Camera capture → Face detection → Feature extraction → Comparison → Threshold-based alert → Operator review → Possible police action.

This reconstruction is more useful than simply saying that police used AI.

It identifies where relevant choices and records may exist.

Identifying the System Boundary

A system boundary defines which components and activities are included in an analysis.

The boundary is not necessarily identical to the software product.

For example, an engineer evaluating facial comparison accuracy may concentrate on the recognition model and its inputs.

A lawyer examining the lawfulness of deployment may need to include watchlist selection, authorisation procedures, human review, and police action.

The appropriate boundary depends on the question.

This creates an important methodological rule:

The legal question should inform the system boundary, rather than allowing the available software documentation to define the entire investigation.

If the legal concern is whether individuals were placed on a watchlist under sufficiently clear criteria, an investigation confined to the recognition model would be incomplete.

If the concern is whether an operator relied on an incorrect alert, the analysis may need to include the interface and human review process.

If the concern is whether the system processed biometric information, the investigation must examine the relevant data operations.

The model is part of the system.

It is not necessarily the whole system.

Identifying Decision Points

During reconstruction, particular attention should be given to decision points.

A decision point is a stage at which a rule, configuration, person, or institution determines what happens next.

In AFR Locate, relevant decision points included the selection of people for the watchlist, the selection of deployment locations, the threshold used to generate possible matches, and the operator's review of an alert.

These decisions are not equivalent.

Some concern institutional policy.

Some concern technical configuration.

Others concern human judgement.

Each may be governed by different requirements.

A system map should therefore distinguish automated operations from human or institutional choices.

Without this distinction, responsibility may be incorrectly attributed to the algorithm.

Stage Three: Evidence Requirements

What Would We Need to See?

After reconstructing the system, the lawyer and engineer can identify the evidence required to examine the legal question.

This stage translates a proposition into a structured evidential request.

Suppose the question concerns whether watchlist inclusion was governed by sufficiently clear criteria.

Relevant evidence may include the applicable policies, operational instructions, watchlist categories, and records showing how those rules were applied.

A facial recognition benchmark would not directly answer that question.

Now suppose the question concerns whether a particular alert was correct.

Relevant evidence may include the original images, reference images, software version, configuration, comparison result, and appropriate expert analysis.

The watchlist policy may provide context, but it cannot by itself establish the correctness of the match.

Finally, suppose the question concerns whether the police investigated demographic performance.

Relevant evidence may include supplier inquiries, evaluation reports, risk assessments, internal decisions, and records of the steps taken to obtain relevant information.

Different propositions require different evidence.

From Legal Duty to Evidence Request

Section 149(1) of the Equality Act 2010 provides a useful example.

It requires public authorities, when exercising their functions, to have due regard to the specified equality objectives.

In the context of Bridges, the relevant inquiry concerned whether the police had adequately investigated the possibility of differential accuracy according to race or sex.

How might a lawyer translate that issue into an evidence request?

The lawyer could seek to establish what information the authority possessed before deployment.

They could examine whether the authority requested demographic performance information from the supplier.

They could identify whether relevant testing had been considered.

They could examine the reasoning recorded when the authority decided to proceed.

These inquiries do not assume that a particular test was universally mandated by section 149.

The legal duty remains one of due regard, applied to the circumstances.

The evidence requests are designed to help determine whether that duty was discharged.

This distinction protects the analysis from turning an illustrative engineering procedure into an invented statutory requirement.

A practical investigation may use an evidence matrix.

An evidence matrix is a structured record connecting questions, relevant system components, required evidence, and the conclusions that the evidence may support.

Stage Four: Technical Evaluation

Does the Evidence Support the Claim?

Collecting technical information is not the same as evaluating it.

A performance report may contain measurements.

A log may contain event records.

A system manual may describe intended behaviour.

The investigator must determine whether the material is reliable and relevant to the proposition under examination.

This requires attention to methodology.

Suppose an institution claims that a facial recognition system has a false positive identification rate of 0.1 per cent.

The number appears precise.

But the lawyer and engineer must ask what was measured.

Was the evaluation concerned with one-to-many identification?

How was a false positive identification defined?

What was the denominator?

What gallery size was used?

Which threshold applied?

Were the test conditions comparable to the deployment?

The National Institute of Standards and Technology's facial recognition evaluations provide examples of task-specific biometric performance measures.

Such evaluations can inform technical assessment.

But a reported rate must be interpreted within the methodology that produced it.

A number without its measurement context is not sufficient technical evidence.

Evaluation Must Match the Intended Use

A model can perform well in one setting and less well in another.

For example, a facial recognition algorithm evaluated using controlled photographs may behave differently when processing images from surveillance cameras under variable lighting.

The technical investigation should therefore examine the relationship between the evaluation conditions and the intended operational environment.

This does not mean that every deployment requires a wholly new scientific study.

The necessary evaluation depends on the system, available evidence, intended use, and applicable requirements.

The methodological principle is narrower:

A technical result should not be generalised beyond the conditions it reasonably supports.

This principle applies to facial recognition, lending models, medical prediction systems, and other AI applications.

Technical Findings Must Include Their Limitations

Suppose an expert concludes that a facial recognition algorithm performed well in a particular benchmark.

A complete technical conclusion should identify the conditions under which that result was obtained.

It should also identify relevant limitations.

For example, the report may not establish performance on a different population.

It may not establish the correctness of an individual alert.

It may not describe the human review process.

These limitations do not necessarily invalidate the report.

They define its evidential scope.

A technically rigorous assessment is not one that claims certainty about everything.

It is one that states clearly what the evidence establishes and what remains uncertain.

Stage Five: Legal Interpretation

What Do the Technical Findings Mean Under the Law?

The fifth stage returns to the legal question.

The lawyer now has a more detailed understanding of the system and the available technical evidence.

But the technical findings must still be interpreted under the applicable legal rules.

Suppose an evaluation reveals that a facial recognition model has different error rates across demographic groups.

What is the legal consequence?

There is no universal answer.

The finding may be relevant to a duty to investigate equality risks.

It may be relevant to the assessment of a system's suitability for deployment.

It may be relevant to a particular discrimination claim.

But the legal conclusion depends on the applicable law, the relevant facts, and the legal elements that must be established.

The technical finding is evidence.

The legal interpretation determines its significance.

Applying Article 8

Consider the first successful ground in Bridges.

The Court of Appeal examined whether the interference with Article 8 rights was in accordance with the law.

The legal analysis concerned the quality of the framework governing the police's use of AFR Locate.

Technical reconstruction helped identify what the system did.

Institutional evidence helped establish the choices available to the police.

The court then assessed whether the relevant framework adequately constrained those choices.

The conclusion was legal.

The court held that the framework failed to provide sufficiently clear limits on important aspects of discretion.

It would be incorrect to describe this conclusion as the result of a facial recognition accuracy test.

The court applied a legal standard to the relevant facts.

This is the fifth stage of the method in practice.

Applying Section 64 of the Data Protection Act 2018

The DPIA issue provides another example.

Section 64(3) identifies matters that a relevant data protection impact assessment must contain.

The Court of Appeal found deficiencies concerning the assessment of risks and the measures envisaged to address them.

Technical understanding was necessary to appreciate the processing and its implications.

But the conclusion depended on the statutory requirements.

A technically sophisticated DPIA would not necessarily be legally adequate if it omitted matters required by law.

Equally, a technically imperfect description does not automatically establish every possible legal breach.

The legal analysis must identify the relevant statutory requirement and explain how the evidence bears upon it.

Applying Section 149 of the Equality Act 2010

The equality issue demonstrates a different relationship between evidence and law.

The Court of Appeal did not establish that AFR Locate had been proved to discriminate.

It held that South Wales Police had failed to take the reasonable steps required to investigate the relevant risk.

The legal conclusion concerned compliance with a procedural duty.

The technical issue concerned the possibility of differential performance.

The connection between them was the authority's obligation to give due regard to the relevant equality objectives.

This illustrates why legal interpretation cannot be replaced by a generic AI fairness score.

The court must identify the applicable legal duty.

The technical evidence must be considered in relation to that duty.

Stage Six: Assurance or Remedy

What Should Follow from the Findings?

The final stage concerns the appropriate response.

Not every investigation begins with an allegation of wrongdoing.

Some are conducted before deployment to establish whether a system is sufficiently controlled.

Others arise after an incident, complaint, regulatory inquiry, or legal challenge.

The appropriate outcome therefore depends on the purpose of the investigation.

An organisation seeking to deploy a system may need additional testing, clearer policies, revised procedures, or stronger documentation.

An institution examining an operational failure may need to correct inaccurate data, modify a configuration, improve human review, or suspend a particular use pending investigation.

A lawyer challenging a decision may seek an appropriate legal remedy.

These are different forms of response.

Assurance: Justifying Confidence with Evidence

An assurance claim is a proposition about a system that an organisation seeks to justify through evidence.

For example:

“The system's identification performance is sufficiently reliable for the defined operational use.”

That claim requires more than a general statement of confidence.

The organisation must explain the intended use, the performance requirements, the evaluation methods, and the results supporting the conclusion.

Another claim might be:

“Human oversight operates effectively.”

Supporting evidence may include procedures, training, interface design, operational records, and monitoring results.

A third claim might concern compliance with a particular legal obligation.

That claim requires identification of the applicable legal standard and evidence relevant to its satisfaction.

The structure can be expressed as:

Claim → Justification → Evidence → Limitations.

This resembles the reasoning used in structured assurance cases.

It does not mean that an organisation can declare itself legally compliant merely by producing an assurance document.

The legal validity of the claim remains subject to the applicable law and relevant decision-making authorities.

The value of the method lies in making the justification explicit and open to examination.

Remedy: Responding to an Established Legal Problem

Where an investigation identifies a legal wrong, the appropriate remedy depends on the legal system and cause of action.

Possible forms of relief may include declarations, orders, damages where legally available, or other remedies provided by the applicable framework.

The remedy must not be inferred from the technical finding alone.

A high error rate does not automatically entitle every affected person to compensation.

An incorrect prediction does not automatically establish all elements of a legal claim.

A breach of a procedural duty may lead to a different remedy from a claim involving compensable loss.

The applicable legal rules determine the consequences.

In Bridges, the Court of Appeal allowed the appeal on three grounds and granted declaratory relief.

The court did not impose a universal prohibition on facial recognition technology.

Nor did it award damages for a proven incorrect identification of Edward Bridges.

The remedy corresponded to the legal findings made in the proceedings.

This illustrates the importance of the final stage.

The investigation should lead to a response justified by the findings, not to a predetermined outcome.

Applying the Complete Method to Bridges

We can now apply the six stages to the case that has guided this chapter.

The following analysis is a reconstruction using the methodology proposed in this book. It is not presented as the procedural sequence formally adopted by the Court of Appeal.

First: Identify the Legal Questions

The principal successful issues concerned the Article 8 requirement that interference be in accordance with the law, the statutory requirements governing the DPIA, and the Public Sector Equality Duty.

Relevant authorities included:

Article 8(1)–(2), European Convention on Human Rights, incorporated through the Human Rights Act 1998.

Section 64(3)(b)–(c), Data Protection Act 2018, as applicable to the processing and litigation.

Section 149(1), Equality Act 2010.

These provisions identified different legal duties.

Second: Reconstruct the System

The system captured facial images, extracted biometric information, compared it with a watchlist, generated possible matches, and presented those matches for human review.

The police also made decisions about watchlist inclusion and deployment locations.

These institutional choices were relevant to the legal inquiry.

Third: Identify the Required Evidence

The Article 8 inquiry required examination of the legal and policy framework governing police discretion.

The DPIA inquiry required examination of the assessment and its treatment of relevant risks and safeguards.

The equality inquiry required examination of the steps taken to investigate possible differential accuracy.

The relevant evidence was therefore not confined to the recognition algorithm.

Fourth: Evaluate the Evidence

The court considered the material relevant to each ground.

Its analysis identified insufficient constraints on important areas of discretion, deficiencies in the DPIA, and inadequate inquiry into the relevant equality risk.

These were findings about the legal framework and institutional conduct.

They were not equivalent to a comprehensive technical benchmark of the recognition software.

Fifth: Apply the Law

The Court of Appeal concluded that the Article 8 interference was not in accordance with the law.

It found non-compliance with the relevant DPIA requirements.

It also found a breach of the Public Sector Equality Duty.

The court rejected the separate proportionality ground and the other data protection ground.

Sixth: Identify the Appropriate Outcome

The appeal succeeded on three grounds.

The court granted declaratory relief.

The judgment did not establish that all facial recognition technology was unlawful or that the particular software had been proved to discriminate.

The remedy and conclusions were confined to the legal findings.

This application demonstrates the value of separating the stages.

It prevents the technical evidence from being treated as the legal conclusion.

It also prevents the legal question from being reduced to a request for the algorithm's source code.

A Second Example: An AI-Assisted Lending Decision

The method should not depend entirely on facial recognition.

Consider a hypothetical bank that uses machine learning to support lending decisions.

An applicant is refused credit.

They believe the decision may have been influenced by an inappropriate variable.

How should the investigation begin?

The first stage is to identify the legal issue under the applicable jurisdiction.

The relevant framework might concern discrimination, data protection, consumer protection, contractual obligations, or another area of law.

The lawyer must identify the actual provisions that apply rather than assuming that the rules governing police facial recognition also govern commercial lending.

The second stage is to reconstruct the lending system.

What data were collected?

What model generated the risk score?

What decision rule converted the score into a recommendation?

Did a human employee review the application?

What other factors influenced the final decision?

The third stage is to identify the evidence required.

This might include the applicant's recorded information, relevant model documentation, the decision threshold, operational records, and the bank's decision procedures.

The fourth stage is technical evaluation.

An expert might examine whether the recorded data were accurate, whether the model behaved as represented, and whether relevant performance differences existed.

The fifth stage is legal interpretation.

The lawyer must determine whether the findings satisfy the elements of the applicable legal claim.

A statistical difference alone does not automatically establish unlawful discrimination.

Nor does a model's predictive accuracy automatically establish that its use was lawful.

The sixth stage concerns the response.

Depending on the findings and applicable law, the response might involve correcting information, reconsidering the application, modifying a procedure, or pursuing an available legal remedy.

The method remains the same.

The legal rules and technical evidence change with the context.

A Third Example: AI in Public Administration

Consider a government agency using AI to prioritise complaints.

The system receives written complaints, summarises their contents, and recommends the department to which each complaint should be referred.

The objective is to improve administrative efficiency.

But suppose a complaint is routed to the wrong department.

The complainant experiences a delay.

Does this establish that the AI system acted unlawfully?

Not by itself.

The investigation must identify the relevant legal duties and the actual decision-making process.

The first question may concern whether the institution was legally required to handle the complaint within a particular period or according to a particular procedure.

The system reconstruction may reveal that the AI model generated a routing recommendation but that a human administrator was responsible for approving the referral.

The evidence requirements may include the original complaint, the model's summary, the routing recommendation, the administrative review, and the subsequent handling record.

Technical evaluation may examine whether the summary omitted material information or whether the routing model misclassified the complaint.

Legal interpretation must then determine whether the established facts amount to a breach of the applicable administrative requirements.

The response may involve correcting the referral, improving the workflow, or adopting additional safeguards.

The important point is that a routing error and a legal violation are not automatically the same proposition.

The relationship must be established.

This example also demonstrates why AI governance is not confined to advanced predictive models.

Systems that summarise documents or recommend administrative actions can affect institutional processes.

Their legal significance depends on their use and consequences.

The Evidence Translation Problem

The three examples reveal a recurring difficulty.

Legal requirements are often expressed in concepts such as fairness, reasonableness, necessity, accountability, and proportionality.

Engineering systems are often described through inputs, outputs, thresholds, error rates, configurations, and performance tests.

A legal requirement does not automatically arrive in the form of a software specification.

It must be interpreted.

Likewise, a technical measurement does not automatically arrive as a legal conclusion.

It must be evaluated.

The translation between these domains is therefore a distinct professional activity.

It requires three forms of discipline.

First, the legal rule must be identified accurately.

Second, the technical question must be formulated in a way that can be investigated.

Third, the resulting evidence must be interpreted without overstating what it establishes.

This is the foundation of Legal–Technical AI Assurance.

The objective is not to replace legal judgement with engineering measurements.

It is to make legal judgement better informed by reliable technical evidence.

Nor is the objective to turn every legal obligation into a numerical score.

Some obligations concern institutional authority, procedure, or judgement and cannot be reduced to a single metric.

Technical testing can support the inquiry.

It cannot eliminate the need for legal interpretation.

The Questions That Change the Investigation

We can now return to the lawyer who began by asking to see the algorithm.

After learning the six-stage method, the lawyer approaches the technical team differently.

They explain the disputed decision.

They identify the relevant legal issue.

They ask the engineers to describe the complete workflow rather than only the model.

They establish which components influenced the outcome.

They identify the records that could reconstruct the event.

They ask which tests are relevant and what those tests can establish.

They then evaluate the findings under the applicable legal rules.

The conversation has changed.

The lawyer is no longer requesting technical information without a defined purpose.

The engineer is no longer explaining the model without understanding the legal question.

Both are working towards the same evidential objective.

This does not eliminate disagreement.

The available records may be incomplete.

Experts may disagree about methodology.

The legal standard may require interpretation.

The institution may contest the factual allegations.

But the structure makes the disagreement more precise.

It identifies which propositions are established, which remain disputed, and which cannot be resolved from the available evidence.

That is a substantial improvement over treating artificial intelligence as either infallible or inherently untrustworthy.

What We Have Learned from One Face

At the beginning of this chapter, a face appeared before a camera.

We followed that face into a technological system.

We learned that the camera captures digital information.

We learned how software detects a face and extracts numerical representations.

We learned how a model compares those representations with reference data.

We examined similarity scores, thresholds, and different kinds of error.

We saw how an alert can influence human judgement and institutional action.

We then examined the legal questions arising from the processing of biometric information, the exercise of police discretion, the assessment of privacy risks, and the investigation of equality concerns.

Finally, we considered the evidence needed to examine those questions and the limits of the conclusions that technical evidence can support.

The journey began with a seemingly simple question:

Can a machine recognise a face?

It ends with a more demanding set of questions.

What was the system designed to recognise?

How did it perform the task?

What evidence supports its output?

Who decided how the output would be used?

Which legal duties governed that use?

What can be established about compliance?

And what should follow when the evidence reveals a problem?

These questions are not limited to facial recognition.

They apply, with appropriate adaptation, to AI systems used in healthcare, finance, employment, public administration, education, and other consequential settings.

The technical components may differ.

The legal regimes may differ.

But the need to connect claims, evidence, and conclusions remains.

The Method We Will Carry Forward

The remainder of this book develops the six-stage method in greater depth.

Part I continues our examination of how intelligent systems learn and how their components contribute to decisions.

Part II examines the evidence hidden in data, the limitations of performance measures, the problems of bias and fairness, and the challenges of explainability and verification.

Part III considers how technical findings can be used in legal reasoning, including questions of privacy, admissibility, responsibility, causation, and remedies.

Part IV turns to governance and assurance.

It examines how legal duties can inform engineering requirements, how AI systems can be audited, and how institutions can construct structured arguments supported by verifiable evidence.

The objective is cumulative.

Each technical concept will help us ask a more precise legal question.

Each legal question will help us identify the evidence that matters.

Each evaluation will help us distinguish what is established from what remains uncertain.

And each conclusion will remain limited by the evidence and law supporting it.

This is not a method for producing automatic legal answers.

It is a method for making legal–technical reasoning more disciplined, transparent, and defensible.

The central proposition of this book can now be stated:

A legal principle becomes operationally meaningful in AI governance when we can identify the conduct it regulates, reconstruct the system through which that conduct occurs, and determine what evidence is needed to assess compliance.

That does not mean every legal question can be resolved by engineering.

It means engineering evidence can be brought into legal reasoning without confusing the roles of the two disciplines.

The lawyer remains responsible for legal analysis.

The engineer remains responsible for technical analysis within their expertise.

The institution remains responsible for the duties imposed upon it.

And the evidence provides the common ground on which their claims can be examined.

We began with a face in the crowd.

We end with a method for asking what happened, what can be proved, and what the law requires.
